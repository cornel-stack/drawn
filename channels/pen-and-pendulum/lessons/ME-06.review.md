## Subscript mapping (ours → source)

Our lesson uses the river-boat frames: **bw** = boat relative to water, **wg** =
water relative to ground, **bg** = boat relative to ground. The source states the
identical rule in two worked examples with different letters:

- Ex 4.14 (plane in a wind): **v_PG = v_PA + v_AG**, P = plane, A = air,
  G = ground (p.180). Mapping A(air)→water, P(plane)→boat, G(ground)→ground gives
  exactly our **v_bg = v_bw + v_wg**. The air is the connecting frame there, as
  the water is for us.
- Ex 4.13 (car relative to truck) and the 1-D train opener give the subscript
  mechanics (coupling frame on the inside, reversal, difference).

So the lesson's symbols are our river-boat relabelling of the source's own
velocity-addition rule; the physics and subscript bookkeeping are unchanged.

## Context confirmed from §4.5

- **Velocity is always relative to a reference frame** (p.177):
  > "When we say an object has a certain velocity, we must state it has a velocity
  > with respect to a given reference frame." And:
  > "all discussion of relative motion must define the reference frames involved."
  This supports the s1/s2 narration ("velocity is always measured relative to
  something").
- **Reversing frames negates the relative velocity** (the 1-D opener, p.177):
  > "if another train passes you at 15 m/s east, your velocity relative to this
  > other train is different from your velocity relative to the ground. Your
  > velocity relative to the other train is 5 m/s west." (15 east vs the other
  > train's frame → 5 west: the difference of ground velocities, with a sign flip
  > on reversal.)

## Equation `addEq` — v_bg = v_bw + v_wg

§4.5 Ex 4.14 *Flying a Plane in a Wind* (p.180):
> "The vector equation is … where P = plane, A = air, and G = ground."
i.e. **v_PG = v_PA + v_AG**. This is our v_bg = v_bw + v_wg with air→water. The
frame-addition rule itself is Eq 4.35 (p.178):
> "The velocity of a particle relative to S is equal to its velocity relative to
> [S′] plus the velocity of [S′] relative to S."
The legend (bg, bw, wg all m/s) is consistent with the source. Verdict: ok.

## Equation `chainEq` — v_AC = v_AB + v_BC

§4.5 **Equation 4.36** (p.178):
> "We can extend Equation 4.35 to any number of reference frames. For particle P
> with velocities in frames A, B, and C, [Eq 4.36]."
The inner frame cancels because, per Fig 4.24 (p.177):
> "When constructing the vector equation, the subscripts for the coupling
> reference frame appear consecutively on the inside. The subscripts on the
> left-hand side of the equation are the same as the two outside subscripts on
> the right-hand side of the equation."
So the shared middle index B (between AB and BC) is the coupling frame that drops
out, leaving AC — exactly the lesson's chain reading. Our boat is A=boat, B=water,
C=ground. Verdict: ok.

## Equation `flipEq` — v_AB = -v_BA

§4.5 Ex 4.13 *Motion of a Car Relative to a Truck* (p.179):
> "Since we have the velocity of the truck with respect to Earth, the negative of
> this vector is the velocity of Earth with respect to the truck"
i.e. **v_ET = -v_TE**: reversing the two frames negates the relative velocity.
The lesson item v_AB = -v_BA states precisely this. Verdict: ok.

## Equation `subEq` — v_AB = v_AG - v_BG

§4.5 Ex 4.13 (p.179): the relative-motion equation sought is v_CT (car w.r.t.
truck), formed by linking car, truck and the common Earth frame:
> "we write the velocities of each with respect to the reference frame of Earth,
> which enables us to form a vector equation that links the car, the truck, and
> Earth to solve for the velocity of the car with respect to the truck."
Then v_CT = v_CE + v_ET = v_CE - v_TE (using flipEq), i.e. the velocity of one
object relative to another is the **difference of their velocities in the common
(ground) frame**. The lesson's v_AB = v_AG - v_BG is this with Earth→ground.
Confirmed too by the 1-D opener (15 east minus the other train's velocity → 5
west). Verdict: ok.

## Problem s4 — ground speed and direction

## Equation `given` — v_bw = 4 m/s (across), v_wg = 3 m/s (down)

River-boat problem of the §4.5 *Check Your Understanding 4.6* type (p.180):

> that is running east at 3.0 m/s. What is the velocity of the boat with respect

Our numbers (4 m/s across, 3 m/s downstream) are **our own** — the source's CYU
uses 4.5 and 3.0 m/s. Verdict: ok.

## Equation `step1` — v_bg = sqrt(4^2 + 3^2)

The across-stream and downstream velocities are perpendicular, so they combine by
Pythagoras — the component method of Ex 4.14 (p.181), which resolves the plane's
ground velocity into independent x and y components:
> "(a) In the x direction we have the following … (b) In the y direction we have
> the following" (p.181).
With a right angle between v_bw (across) and v_wg (down), the resultant magnitude
is sqrt(v_bw^2 + v_wg^2). Recomputed below. Verdict: ok.

## Equation `answer` — v_bg = 5 m/s

sqrt(4^2 + 3^2) = 5.0 m/s (the 3-4-5 right triangle). Recomputed below. The
`check:` expr `(4**2 + 3**2)**0.5` = 5.0 (boxed value 5, tol 1 %). Verdict: ok.

## Equation `step2` — tan φ = 3/4 ⇒ φ ≈ 36.9°

Direction from the component ratio, as in Ex 4.14 where the heading angle follows
from the x/y components (p.180–181):
> "The diagram will also tell us the angle the plane's velocity makes with north".
The downstream angle from straight-across has tan φ = v_wg / v_bw = 3/4, so
φ = atan(0.75) ≈ 36.87°, matching the lesson's "about thirty-seven degrees".
Recomputed below. Verdict: ok.
