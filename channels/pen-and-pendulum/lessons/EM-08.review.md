# Fact-check — EM-08 (RC circuits: charging and discharging, the differential equation solved visually)

Source: OpenStax *University Physics Volume 2* (CC BY 4.0), §10.5 *RC Circuits*
(book pp. 440–445, PDF pp. 452–457). Extracted text:
`sources/text/university-physics-vol2/086-10-5-rc-circuits.md`.

> Note on quoting: the OpenStax PDF renders its displayed (numbered) equations as
> images, so the symbolic glyphs are absent from the extracted text — only the
> surrounding prose that introduces or labels each one survives (same situation
> as `EM-06`/`EM-07.review.md`). Where that is so, the quoted line is the
> sentence that defines or labels the equation and the symbolic form is confirmed
> against the standard OpenStax equation number (Eq 10.8 the loop-rule ODE, Eq
> 10.9 the charge/time-constant, Eq 10.11 discharging). The blank lines in the
> extracted text are exactly where the image-equations sat (e.g. after "This

## Equation `tauEq` — τ = RC

§10.5, Charging a Capacitor, Eq **10.9** (p.441), quoted:

> "The units of RC are seconds, units of time. This quantity is known as the time
> constant:"

(the displayed **τ = RC** sat on the blank line after the colon). The board's
`\tau = RC` with the legend τ [s], R [Ω], C [F] matches exactly; the
units-of-seconds claim is quoted verbatim. ✔

## Equation `deEq` — RC dV/dt + V = ε (loop rule → first-order ODE)

§10.5, Charging a Capacitor, Eq **10.8** (p.441), quoted:

> "We can use Kirchhoff's loop rule to understand the charging of the capacitor.
> This results in the equation [ε = IR + q/C]. This equation can be used to model
> the charge as a function of time as the capacitor charges."

and the two substitutions that convert it to the board form, same page:

> "Capacitance is defined as [C = q/V], so the voltage across the capacitor is
> [V = q/C]. Using Ohm's law, the potential drop across the resistor is [IR], and
> the current is defined as [I = dq/dt]."

The loop rule ε = IR + q/C with I = dq/dt = C dV/dt and q/C = V rearranges to the
board's **R C dV/dt + V = ε** (equivalently R dq/dt + q/C = ε). The symbolic ODE
is confirmed against the standard OpenStax Eq 10.8; the board writes it in the
capacitor voltage, which the source does explicitly for V in Figure 10.39(c). ✔

## Equation `chargeEq` — V = V₀(1 − e^{−t/RC})

§10.5, Charging a Capacitor, p.441, quoted:

> "Simplifying results in an equation for the charge on the charging capacitor as
> a function of time:"

(the displayed **q(t) = Cε(1 − e^{−t/RC})** sat on the blank line after the
colon), with the maximum-charge identification, same page:

> "as time approaches infinity, the exponential goes to zero, so the charge
> approaches the maximum charge [q0 = Cε] and has units of coulombs."

Dividing by C (V = q/C, and V₀ = q₀/C = ε) gives the board's **V = V₀(1 −
e^{−t/RC})**. The voltage form itself is stated for the capacitor, p.442:

> "The voltage difference across the capacitor increases as [V = ε(1 −
> e^{−t/RC})]."

At t = 0 the capacitor is empty; as t → ∞ the exponential fades and V → V₀ — the
narration's description matches the source's "approaches the maximum". ✔

**Discharging** (narration claim "pure decaying exponential", 37% after one τ) is
in the source, §10.5, Discharging a Capacitor, Eq **10.11** (p.442), quoted:

> "Using Kirchhoff's loop rule to analyze the circuit as the capacitor discharges
> results in the equation [IR + q/C = 0], which simplifies to [dq/dt = −q/RC].
> Using the definition of current and integrating the loop equation yields an
> equation for the charge on the capacitor as a function of time: [q(t) = q0
> e^{−t/RC}]. … the charge decreases exponentially from the initial charge,
> approaching zero as time approaches infinity."

so q = q₀ e^{−t/RC} ⇒ V = V₀ e^{−t/RC}; at t = τ the factor is e^{−1} = 0.368,
i.e. 37% — consistent with the board (discharging curve, red). ✔

## 63% / 37% / five-time-constant claims (narration)

The 63% figure is the source's own value, §10.5 (p.441), quoted:

> "At time [t = RC], the charge is equal to [0.632] of the maximum charge [q0]."

1 − 1/e = 0.6321 (charging reaches 63% after one τ); 1/e = 0.3679 (discharging
falls to 37% after one τ). The "all but full after about five τ" rule of thumb
(1 − e^{−5} = 0.9933, > 99%) is the standard engineering heuristic; the source
states the exponential approaches its limit as t → ∞ (p.441, "as time approaches
infinity … the charge approaches the maximum charge") — marked **standard**,
consistent with the source. ✔

## Problem `s4` — time constant (equations `given`, `step1`, `answer`)

Self-contained: R = 2000 Ω and C = 1×10⁻³ F in series, find τ = RC (§10.5 Eq
10.9, p.441). Numbers are our own. Recomputation:

```python
R = 2000.0   # ohm
C = 1e-3     # F
tau = R * C
print("tau = R*C = 2000 * 1e-3 =", tau, "s")
```

Output:

```
tau = R*C = 2000 * 1e-3 = 2.0 s
```

Board `step1` substitutes (2000)(1×10⁻³); `answer` boxes **2 s** — matches to
0.00 %. Units: Ω·F = Ω·(C/V) = Ω·(A·s/V) = (V/A)·(A·s/V) = s. ✔

## Problem `s5` — voltage after one time constant (equations `given2`, `step2`, `answer2`)

Self-contained: V₀ = 10 V charging, find V at t = τ using V = V₀(1 − e^{−t/RC})
with t/RC = 1 (§10.5 p.441–442; 63% figure quoted above). Numbers are our own.
Recomputation:

```python
import numpy as np
V0 = 10.0
em1 = np.exp(-1)
V = V0 * (1 - em1)
print("e^-1 =", em1)
print("1 - e^-1 =", 1 - em1)
print("V = 10*(1 - e^-1) =", V, "V")
```

Output:

```
e^-1 = 0.36787944117144233
1 - e^-1 = 0.6321205588285577
V = 10*(1 - e^-1) = 6.321205588285577 V
```

e^{−1} = 0.3679 (narration "about nought point three seven"), 1 − e^{−1} =
0.6321 (narration "nought point six three"); V = 6.321 V. Board `step2`
substitutes 10(1 − e^{−1}); `answer2` boxes **≈ 6.3 V** — the check value 6.32
matches to 0.019 %. This is the 63% rule in numbers, as the source states
(p.441). ✔
