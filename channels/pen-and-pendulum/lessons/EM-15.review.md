## Equation `peakEq` — ε₀ = NBAω (scene s2)

§13.6, Eq **13.16** (p.572, PDF p.584), quoted:

> "This can also be expressed as [ε = ε₀ sin ωt] where [ε₀ = NBAω] is the peak
> emf, since the maximum value of [sin ωt = 1]."

and the plain-words confirmation (p.572):

> "The fact that the peak emf is [ε₀ = NBAω] makes good sense. The greater the
> number of coils, the larger their area, and the stronger the field, the
> greater the output voltage. It is interesting that the faster the generator is
> spun (greater ω), the greater the emf."

Board's `ε₀ = NBAω` matches Eq 13.16 exactly. Legend N (turns), A (coil area,
m²), ω (angular speed, rad/s) are the three variable factors. The s2 narration —
a coil rotating steadily in a field produces a sinusoidal voltage, "fastest when
the coil is edge-on … zero for an instant when it is face-on", "it is exactly
the alternating-current voltage that comes out of every wall socket", "the
beating heart of every power station … steam, water, or wind" — is directly
supported by §13.6 Figure 13.29 ("it now seems reasonable that ac voltage is
sinusoidal") and p.572–573 ("The source of mechanical energy that turns the coil
can be falling water (hydropower), steam produced by the burning of fossil fuels,
or the kinetic energy of wind … the basic principle of all power that is sent
through our electrical grids"). ✔

## Equation `emfEq` — ε = NBAω sin(ωt) (scene s3)

§13.6, Eq **13.15** (p.572, PDF p.584), quoted:

> "Noting that the area of the loop is [A = lw] and allowing for N loops, we find
> that [ε = NBAω sin ωt]. This is the emf induced in a generator coil of N turns
> and area A rotating at a constant angular velocity ω in a uniform magnetic
> field B."

(the displayed **ε = NBAω sin ωt** sat on the blank line after "we find that" —
Eq 13.15). Board's `ε = NBAω sin(ωt)` matches exactly. The s3 derivation route
is §13.6, p.571–572: motional emf on the side wires, velocity component v sin θ,
θ = ωt, assembling to the sinusoidal emf. The narration's version — flux goes as
NBA cos ωt, Faraday's law ε = −dΦ/dt, "the derivative of cosine is minus sine, so
the e m f is N B A omega times the sine of omega t" — is the standard
cos→(−sin) differentiation that yields Eq 13.15 (equivalent to the source's
motional-emf route; both give the same NBAω sin ωt). ✔

## Back emf narration (scene s3, item `backEq`)

Item `backEq` (ε_back opposes the supply) is a text-plus-label item, not a free
equation, so the scaffold does not require it in the `equations` list; its
content is fact-checked here. §13.6, Back Emf, p.573–574, quoted:

> "Lenz's law tells us the emf opposes any change, so that the input emf that
> powers the motor is opposed by the motor's self-generated emf, called the back
> emf of the motor."

> "The back emf is zero when the motor is first turned on, meaning that the coil
> receives the full driving voltage and the motor draws maximum current when it
> is on but not turning. As the motor turns faster, the back emf grows, always
> opposing the driving emf, and reduces both the voltage across the coil and the
> amount of current it draws."

> "When a motor first comes on, it draws more current than when it runs at its
> normal operating speed … If the motor runs at too low a speed, the larger
> current can overheat it (via resistive power in the coil) perhaps even burning
> it out."

Board's `ε_back opposes the supply` and the s3 narration — "a motor is a
generator run in reverse", the switch-on surge with no back emf, the current
dropping to a safe running value as the motor speeds up, and the overheating of a
motor labouring under heavy load — match this passage point for point. ✔

## Eddy-current narration (scene s3)

§13.5, quoted:

> "If motional emf can cause a current in the conductor, we refer to that current
> as an eddy current." (p.566, PDF p.578)

> "As it enters from the left, flux increases, setting up an eddy current
> (Faraday's law) in the counterclockwise direction (Lenz's law) … it
> experiences a force opposing its motion." (p.566, induced loops in a solid
> conductor from changing flux, opposing motion — magnetic braking)

> "Other major applications of eddy currents appear in metal detectors and
> braking systems in trains and roller coasters … The vehicle has metal fins
> (normally containing copper) that pass through the magnetic field, slowing the
> vehicle down." (p.568)

> "Induction cooktops have electromagnets under their surface. The magnetic field
> is varied rapidly, producing eddy currents in the base of the pot, causing the
> pot and its contents to increase in temperature." (p.569 — induction heating)

> "If eddy currents are to be avoided in conductors, then they must be slotted or
> constructed of thin layers of conducting material separated by insulating
> sheets." (p.567)

and §13.6, p.574:

> "Eddy currents in iron cores of motors can cause troublesome energy losses.
> These are usually minimized by constructing the cores out of thin, electrically
> insulated sheets of iron."

The s3 narration — changing flux in a solid lump of metal induces "whole
swirling loops of current", which "dissipate energy as heat", used for "magnetic
brakes that slow trains and rollercoasters" and "induction hobs that heat a pan
directly", and a nuisance in transformer/motor cores where "the wasted heat is
suppressed by building the core from thin insulated laminations that break the
eddy loops apart" — matches §13.5/§13.6 exactly. ✔

## Problem `s4` — peak emf of a rotating generator coil (equations `given`, `step1`, `answer`)

Self-contained application of §13.6 Eq 13.16 ε₀ = NBAω. Numbers are our own:
N = 100, B = 0.2 T, A = 0.01 m², ω = 50 rad/s (the source's Example 13.9 uses a
200-turn, 5.00-cm-radius coil in 0.80 T over 15.0 ms; numbers changed as
required). Recomputation:

```python
import numpy as np
N, B, A, w = 100, 0.2, 0.01, 50
peak = N * B * A * w
print("peak emf = N*B*A*w =", N, "*", B, "*", A, "*", w, "=", peak, "V")
print("check expr 100*0.2*0.01*50 =", 100 * 0.2 * 0.01 * 50)
print("boxed 10; rel diff =", abs(peak - 10) / 10)
```

output:

```
peak emf = N*B*A*w = 100 * 0.2 * 0.01 * 50 = 10.0 V
check expr 100*0.2*0.01*50 = 10.0
boxed 10; rel diff = 0.0
```

Board `step1` substitutes (100)(0.2)(0.01)(50) and `answer` boxes **10 V**.
Matches the boxed value exactly (rel diff 0.0). Units:
(dimensionless)(T)(m²)(rad/s) = T·m²/s = Wb/s = V. The narration ("ten volts …
the crest of the sine wave; the voltage swings between plus and minus ten", and
"spin it twice as fast and the peak doubles") is the ∝ ω dependence of
ε₀ = NBAω. ✔

## Problem `s5` — RMS voltage of the generator (equations `given2`, `step2`, `answer2`)

Application of the standard sine-wave RMS relation ε_rms = ε₀/√2. This relation
is **not** in §13.6 — it is introduced in OpenStax Ch. 15 *Alternating-Current
Circuits* §15.3 *RMS Current and Voltage* (V_rms = V₀/√2, I_rms = I₀/√2). §13.6
establishes only that the generator output is sinusoidal (p.572: "it now seems
reasonable that ac voltage is sinusoidal"; Example 13.9 Significance: "similar to
the 120 V used in household power"), so the lesson's RMS cross-reference is
correct in kind and cited to the standard AC relation. Numbers are our own:
ε₀ = 10 V (carried from s4). Recomputation:

```python
import numpy as np
rms = 10 / np.sqrt(2)
print("rms = peak/sqrt(2) = 10/sqrt(2) =", rms, "V")
print("check expr 10/np.sqrt(2) =", 10 / np.sqrt(2))
print("boxed 7.07; rel diff =", abs(rms - 7.07) / 7.07)
```

output:

```
rms = peak/sqrt(2) = 10/sqrt(2) = 7.071067811865475 V
check expr 10/np.sqrt(2) = 7.071067811865475
boxed 7.07; rel diff = 0.00015103421010952375
```

Board `step2` substitutes 10/√2 and `answer2` boxes **≈ 7.07 V**. Matches the
boxed value to within 0.015 % (well inside tol 1 %). Units: (V)/(dimensionless) =
V. The narration ("this is the number quoted for mains electricity — when a
socket is called two hundred and thirty volts, that is the root-mean-square,
while the peak is actually higher by a factor of root two … the value that
delivers the same heating power as a steady direct voltage of the same size") is
the standard definition of RMS for a sine wave. ✔
