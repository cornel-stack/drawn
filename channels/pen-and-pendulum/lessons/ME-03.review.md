# Fact-check — ME-03 (Free fall and the velocity-time graph)

Source: OpenStax *University Physics Volume 1* (CC BY 4.0), Chapter 3 *Motion
Along a Straight Line*, §3.5 *Free Fall*. Extracted text:
`sources/text/university-physics-vol1/025-3-5-free-fall.md` (the header marks it
PDF pp. 138–143; the printed page numbers in the running feet are 126–131, which
is what the lesson's `source.pages: 126-131` records and what is cited below).

> Note on quoting: the OpenStax PDF renders its displayed equations as images,
> so the equation glyphs (Eq 3.15, 3.16, 3.17 and the value of g) are not present
> in the extracted text — only the surrounding prose and the equation numbers
> printed in the margin. Where that is so, the quoted line is the sentence that
> introduces or labels the equation, and the symbolic form is confirmed against
> the standard OpenStax equation number.

## Context confirmed from §3.5

- **Free fall = motion under gravity alone, air resistance negligible** (p.126):
  > "An interesting application of Equation 3.4 through Equation 3.14 is called
  > free fall, which describes the motion of an object falling in a gravitational
  > field, such as near the surface of Earth". And (p.127):
  > "For the ideal situations of these first few chapters, an object falling
  > without air resistance or friction is defined to be in free fall."
- **All objects fall at the same rate regardless of mass (Galileo)** (p.126):
  > "if air resistance and friction are negligible, then in a given location all
  > objects fall toward the center of Earth with the same constant acceleration,
  > independent of their mass. … Until Galileo Galilei (1564–1642) proved
  > otherwise, people believed that a heavier object has a greater acceleration
  > in a free fall. We now know this is not the case." This is the hammer/feather
  > claim in the s1 title and the Galileo narration in s2.
- **g = 9.8 m/s^2 and the 9.78–9.83 range** (p.127):
  > "its magnitude is given its own symbol, g. It is constant at any given
  > location on Earth and has the average value [9.8 m/s2]. Although g varies from
  > 9.78 m/s2 to 9.83 m/s2, depending on latitude, altitude, underlying geological
  > formations, and local topography, let's use an average value of 9.8 m/s2".
- **Sign convention (up positive => a = -g)** (p.127):
  > "whether acceleration a in the kinematic equations has the value +g or −g
  > depends on how we define our coordinate system. If we define the upward
  > direction as positive, then [a = -g]". Repeated in the Kinematic Equations box
  > (p.128): "We assume here that acceleration equals −g (with the positive
  > direction upward)."
- **At the top of a throw v = 0 but a is still -g** (p.130, Example 3.15(d)):
  > "d. The acceleration is 9.8 m/s2 everywhere, even when the velocity is zero at
  > the top of the path. Although the velocity is zero at the top, it is changing
  > at the rate of 9.8 m/s2 downward." This is exactly the lesson's thumbnail hook
  > and the warning narrated in s3 and s5.

## Equation `vEq` — v = v0 - g t

§3.5 Kinematic Equations box, **Equation 3.15** (p.128):
> "KINEMATIC EQUATIONS FOR OBJECTS IN FREE FALL — We assume here that
> acceleration equals −g (with the positive direction upward). … 3.15"
Equation 3.15 is the velocity equation v = v0 + a t specialised to a = -g, i.e.
v = v0 - g t. The lesson item `v = v_0 - g\,t` matches. Verdict: ok.

## Equation `gval` — g = 9.8 m/s^2

§3.5 "Gravity" (p.127), quoted in full above: average value 9.8 m/s^2, range
9.78–9.83 m/s^2. The lesson item `g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2` matches. Verdict: ok.

## Equation `yEq` — y = y0 + v0 t - 1/2 g t^2

§3.5 Kinematic Equations box, **Equation 3.16** (p.128). Eq 3.16 is the position
equation y = y0 + v0 t + 1/2 a t^2 specialised to a = -g, i.e.
y = y0 + v0 t - 1/2 g t^2. Confirmed by its use in Example 3.14 (p.128):
> "To find the time when the position is −98 m, we use Equation 3.16".
The lesson item `y = y_0 + v_0\,t - \tfrac{1}{2}\,g\,t^2` matches. Verdict: ok.

## Equation `vSqEq` — v^2 = v0^2 - 2 g (y - y0)

§3.5 Kinematic Equations box, **Equation 3.17** (p.128). Eq 3.17 is the timeless
equation v^2 = v0^2 + 2 a (y - y0) specialised to a = -g, i.e.
v^2 = v0^2 - 2 g (y - y0). Confirmed by Example 3.15(b) (p.130):
> "b. At the maximum height, [v = 0]. … Equation 3.17 gives [the maximum height]"
and Example 3.16's use for max height/velocity (p.131). The lesson item
`v^2 = v_0^2 - 2\,g\,(y - y_0)` matches. Verdict: ok.
