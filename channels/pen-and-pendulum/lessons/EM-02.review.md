## Equations

### `Edef` — E = F/q  (electric field = force per unit charge)
§5.4 "Defining a Field", Eq **5.4** (p.184–185), quoted:
> "Fortunately, it is possible to define a quantity, called the electric field,
> which is independent of the test charge. It only depends on the configuration
> of the source charges, and once found, allows us to calculate the force on any
> test charge."

And on units (p.185):
> "notice the units of the electric field: From [E = F/q], the units of E are
> newtons per coulomb, N/C, that is, the electric field applies a force on each
> unit charge."

The displayed Eq 5.4 (image) is E = F/q. The "independent of the test charge"
claim the lesson stresses is stated verbatim. ✔

### `Epoint` — E = kQ/r², radially outward (+) / inward (−)
§5.4 Example 5.3 "The E-Field of an Atom" (p.187), quoted:
> "The electric field is calculated by [E]. Since there is only one source charge
> (the nucleus), this expression simplifies to [E = kQ/r²] … The direction of E
> is radially away from the nucleus in all directions."

Direction convention confirmed by the boxed rule §5.4 p.186:
> "By convention, all electric fields point away from positive source charges and
> point toward negative source charges."

So E = kQ/r², outward for positive and inward for negative. ✔

### `Enet` — E_net = Σ Eᵢ = E₁ + E₂ + ⋯  (superposition of fields)
§5.4 "Superposition", Eq **5.4** (p.186), quoted:
> "Yet another experimental fact about the field is that it obeys the
> superposition principle … We can repeat this process, calculating the field of
> each individual source charge, independently of the existence of any of the
> other charges. The total electric field, then, is the vector sum of all these
> fields. That, in essence, is what Equation 5.4 says."

The lesson's "fields obey superposition because the forces they come from do" is
exactly the §5.4 derivation of Eq 5.4 by dividing the superposed force (Eq 5.2)
by q. ✔

### `rEq` — r = √(z² + R²)  (distance from a ring element to the axial point)
§5.5 Example 5.7 "Electric Field due to a Ring of Charge" (p.194), quoted:
> "A general element of the arc between θ and θ+dθ is of length Rdθ and therefore
> contains a charge equal to λRdθ. The element is at a distance of [√(z²+R²)] from
> P, the angle is [θ], and therefore the electric field is [Eq 5.13]."

The bracketed distance (image) is √(z²+R²), the hypotenuse of the right triangle
from the ring centre (axial leg z) to the rim (radial leg R) in Fig 5.24. ✔

### `cosEq` — cosθ = z/√(z² + R²)  (axial fraction; sideways parts cancel)
§5.5 Example 5.7, p.194 (same passage) and the general symmetry statement §5.5
Example 5.8 (p.195) / Example 5.5 (p.192), quoted:
> "The symmetry of the situation … implies the horizontal (x)-components of the
> field cancel, so that the net field points in the z-direction."

and §5.5 Example 5.8 (p.196):
> "by symmetry, the horizontal components cancel and the field is entirely in the
> vertical … direction. The vertical component of the electric field is extracted
> by multiplying by [cosθ]."

For the ring, cosθ = z/r = z/√(z²+R²); for every element there is a diametrically
opposite one whose radial field cancels, leaving only the axial fraction. ✔

### `Ecos` — E = (kQ/r²)·cosθ  (sum of axial contributions)
§5.5 Example 5.7, p.194 — the field of each element is kdq/r² and only its axial
cosθ component survives (per the symmetry quote above). Because r and θ are the
**same for every element** of the ring, both factor out of the sum and Σdq = Q,
giving E = (kQ/r²)cosθ before substitution. This is the intermediate line of Eq
5.13's derivation. ✔

### `ringEq` — E = kQz/(z² + R²)^{3/2}  (on-axis field of a ring), Eq 5.13
§5.5 Example 5.7, Eq **5.13** (p.194). Substituting r = √(z²+R²) and
cosθ = z/√(z²+R²) into E = (kQ/r²)cosθ gives
E = kQz/(z²+R²)^{3/2}. Limiting cases confirmed by §5.5 Example 5.7 Significance
(p.194):
> "As usual, symmetry simplified this problem … Also, when we take the limit of
> [z ≫ R], we find that [E = kQ/z²] as we expect."

and z = 0 ⇒ numerator z = 0 ⇒ E = 0 (field vanishes at the centre, as every
element's contribution is cancelled by the one across the ring). Both limits as
stated in the lesson. ✔

## Field-line rules (§5.6, narration in s3)
§5.6 "PROBLEM-SOLVING STRATEGY: Drawing Electric Field Lines" (p.201), quoted:
> "1. Electric field lines either originate on positive charges or come in from
> infinity, and either terminate on negative charges or extend out to infinity …
> 3. At every point in space, the field vector at that point is tangent to the
> field line at that same point. 4. The field line density at any point in space
> is proportional to … the magnitude of the field at that point … 5. Field lines
> can never cross … if two field lines crossed at some point, that would imply
> that the electric field was pointing in two different directions at a single
> point."

The lesson's four rules (start on +, end on −; field is tangent; crowding = strong
/ spreading = weak; never cross) match §5.6 rules 1, 3, 4, 5 exactly. ✔
