# Fact-check — EM-22

Source: OpenStax *University Physics Volume 2* §16.3 "Energy Carried by
Electromagnetic Waves" (CC BY 4.0), PDF pages 683–686, extracted to
`sources/text/university-physics-vol2/141-16-3-energy-carried-by-electromagnetic-waves.md`.
The extracted markdown renders the numbered equation bodies as placeholders (the
OCR dropped the display equations), so each equation below is confirmed by the
surrounding prose and its OpenStax equation number (16.27–16.32).

## Equation `poyntingEq`  — `S = (1/mu0) E x B`

> "The energy per unit area per unit time passing through a plane perpendicular
> to the wave, called the energy flux and denoted by S, can be calculated by
> dividing the energy by the area A and the time interval. … More generally, the
> flux of energy through any surface also depends on the orientation of the
> surface. To take the direction into account, we introduce a vector, called the
> **Poynting vector**, with the following definition:" — §16.3, Eq 16.27, p.672.

> "The cross-product of E and B points in the direction perpendicular to both
> vectors. To confirm that the direction of S is that of wave propagation …" —
> §16.3, p.672.

The defining relation is **S = (1/μ₀) E × B** (OpenStax Eq 16.27). The lesson's
`poyntingEq` = `\vec S = (1/μ0) \vec E × \vec B` matches exactly, including the
claim that its magnitude is the intensity (power/area) and its direction is the
direction of energy propagation. Verdict: **ok**.

## Equation `magEq`  — `S = EB/mu0`

> "One more expression for [the intensity] in terms of both electric and
> magnetic field strengths is useful. Substituting the fact that [E = cB], the
> previous expression becomes" — §16.3, Eq 16.32, p.673.

The magnitude form **S = EB/μ₀** follows directly from S = (1/μ₀)|E × B| with
E ⟂ B (so |E × B| = EB). The narration's statement "the fields are
perpendicular, so the cross product is just their product" is correct for a
plane EM wave. OpenStax gives the time-averaged version (Eq 16.32) with the same
EB/μ₀ structure; the instantaneous magnitude S = EB/μ₀ is the direct consequence
of the Eq 16.27 definition. Consistency check (E = cB): see the Python block
below — ½ E₀B₀/μ₀ = 13.26 W/m² agrees with ½ c ε₀ E₀² = 13.28 W/m². Verdict:
**ok**.

## Equation `avgEq`  — `I = (1/2) c eps0 E0^2`

> "The time average of the energy flux is the **intensity I** of the
> electromagnetic wave and is the power per unit area." — §16.3, p.672 (Eq 16.29
> context).

> "The intensity of light moving at speed c in vacuum is then found to be
> [I = (1/2) c ε₀ E₀²] … in terms of the maximum electric field strength E₀
> which is also the electric field amplitude." — §16.3, Eq 16.31, p.673.

The lesson's `avgEq` = `I = (1/2) c ε0 E0^2` is OpenStax Eq 16.31 in structure.
The narration correctly describes it as the time-averaged intensity ⟨S⟩
("average over a cycle … the average intensity is the peak electric field
squared, times the electric constant and the speed of light, over two").
Verdict: **ok**.

## Note on `circuitEq` (not a gate equation)

`circuitEq` = `E ∥ wire, B ↺  ⇒  S inward` contains no `=` sign, so the gate does
not treat it as an equation (correctly — it is a geometric statement, not a
formula) and it is absent from the frontmatter list. The physics is nonetheless
verified: along a current-carrying resistive wire the electric field E points
along the wire (driving the current), the magnetic field B circles the wire
(Ampère's law), and S = (1/μ₀) E × B therefore points radially **inward**.
Integrating that inward flux over the wire's lateral surface yields exactly the
dissipated power I²R. This is the standard textbook application of the Poynting
vector (energy enters a resistor radially from the surrounding field); it is
**consistent with S = E × B/μ₀** and is not spelled out in §16.3, which treats
free-wave propagation. The claim as stated in the lesson is physically correct.

## Problem `s4`

```python
c = 3e8
eps0 = 8.85e-12
E0 = 100
I = 0.5 * c * eps0 * E0**2
print("I =", I, "W/m^2")
print("boxed 13.3; within 2%?", abs(I - 13.3) / 13.3 <= 0.02)

# cross-check via S = EB/mu0 with E0 = c*B0 (OpenStax Eq 16.32)
import math
mu0 = 4 * math.pi * 1e-7
B0 = E0 / c
print("(1/2) E0 B0 / mu0 =", 0.5 * E0 * B0 / mu0, "W/m^2")
```

output:
```
I = 13.275 W/m^2
boxed 13.3; within 2%? True
(1/2) E0 B0 / mu0 = 13.262911924324612 W/m^2
```

check `expr: 0.5 * 3e8 * 8.85e-12 * 100**2` = 13.275, boxed value 13.3,
tol 2% → agreement 0.19% < 2%. **ok**.
