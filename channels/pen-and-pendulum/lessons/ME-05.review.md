# Fact-check — ME-05 (Why circular motion needs a force: centripetal acceleration derived)

Source: OpenStax *University Physics Volume 1* (CC BY 4.0), Chapter 4 *Motion in
Two and Three Dimensions*, §4.4 *Uniform and Nonuniform Circular Motion*.
Extracted text:
`sources/text/university-physics-vol1/033-4-4-uniform-and-nonuniform-circular-motion.md`
(the header marks it PDF pp. 182–188; the printed running-foot page numbers are
170–176, which is what the lesson's `source.pages: 170-176` records and what is
cited below).

> Note on quoting: the OpenStax PDF renders its displayed equations and most
> symbols as images, so the equation glyphs (Eq 4.27 for a_c, and the
> similar-triangle relation) are not present in the extracted text — only the
> surrounding prose and the margin equation numbers. Where that is so, the quoted
> line is the sentence that introduces or labels the equation, and the symbolic
> form is confirmed against the standard OpenStax equation number (Eq 4.27).

## Context confirmed from §4.4

- **Uniform circular motion = constant speed on a circular path** (p.170):
  > "Uniform circular motion is a specific type of motion in which an object
  > travels in a circle with a constant speed." This supports the s2 narration
  > ("moves round it at constant speed — uniform circular motion").
- **An object can accelerate even at constant speed, because velocity is a vector
  whose direction changes** (p.170):
  > "In one-dimensional kinematics, objects with a constant speed have zero
  > acceleration. However, in two- and three-dimensional kinematics, even if the
  > speed is a constant, a particle can have acceleration if it moves along a
  > curved trajectory such as a circle. In this case the velocity vector is
  > changing".
  And (p.170):
  > "It is remarkable that points on these rotating objects are actually
  > accelerating, although the rotation rate is a constant." This is the lesson's
  > whole thesis (s2: "velocity is a vector, so if its direction changes, the
  > velocity is changing, and a changing velocity is an acceleration") and the
  > thumbnail hook "Constant speed, still accelerating".
- **Velocity is tangent, constant magnitude, direction-only change** (p.170):
  > "The velocity vector has constant magnitude and is tangent to the path as it
  > changes from [v(t)] to [v(t+Δt)] changing its direction only." Supports the s2
  > narration ("always tangent to the circle … Its length never changes … but its
  > direction turns continually").
- **The acceleration points toward the centre — "centre-seeking"** (pp.171):
  > "Since [v] is tangent to the circle, the acceleration [a_c] points toward the
  > center of the circle." And:
  > "This is a radial acceleration and is called the centripetal acceleration,
  > which is why we give it the subscript c. The word centripetal comes from the
  > Latin words centrum (meaning 'center') and petere (meaning 'to seek'), and
  > thus takes the meaning 'center seeking.'" Supports s2 ("points straight at the
  > centre … centripetal, meaning centre-seeking").

## Equation `acEq` — a_c = v^2 / r

§4.4 Centripetal Acceleration, **Equation 4.27** (p.171). The text derives the
magnitude from the similar-triangle relation and concludes (p.171):
> "Summarizing, a particle moving in a circle at a constant speed has an
> acceleration with magnitude [a_c = v^2/r]" (margin **Eq 4.27**, p.171).
The lesson item `a_c = \dfrac{v^2}{r}` matches exactly, with the legend
(a_c m/s^2, v m/s, r m) consistent with the source. Verdict: ok.

## Equation `triEq` — Δv / v = Δs / r

§4.4 velocity-triangle / similar-triangles derivation (pp.170–171):
> "Since the velocity vector [v] is perpendicular to the position vector [r], the
> triangles formed by the position vectors … and the velocity vectors … are
> similar. Furthermore, since … the two triangles are isosceles. From these facts
> we can make the assertion [Δv/v = Δs/r]".
(The bracketed relation is the equation rendered as an image in the margin just
before "We can find the magnitude of the acceleration from …".) The source's Δs
is the arc/chord length and r the radius; the lesson item
`\dfrac{\Delta v}{v} = \dfrac{\Delta s}{r}` matches the stated proportion. The s3
narration ("each velocity is at a right angle to its radius, the two triangles
are similar") matches the quoted perpendicularity/similarity argument. Verdict:
ok.

## Equation `omegaEq` — a_c = r ω^2 (with v = r ω)

§4.4 *Equations of Motion for Uniform Circular Motion* (pp.173–174). The section
differentiates the position function: the velocity has magnitude v = r ω (p.173):
> "It can be shown from Figure 4.20 that the velocity vector is tangential to the
> circle at the location of the particle, with magnitude [v = r ω]."
and the acceleration (second derivative), Eq 4.29 region (p.174):
> "From this equation we see that the acceleration vector has magnitude [r ω^2]
> and is directed opposite the position vector, toward the origin".
Combining v = r ω with a_c = v^2/r gives a_c = (rω)^2/r = r ω^2, matching both the
differentiation result and the lesson item `a_c = r\,\omega^2 \quad (v = r\,\omega)`.
Verdict: ok.

## Equation `forceEq` — F = m a_c = m v^2 / r

The force identity is Newton's second law F = m a (OpenStax Ch 5) applied to the
§4.4 centripetal acceleration a_c = v^2/r (Eq 4.27, p.171). §4.4 itself does not
state the force (centripetal force is Ch 6), but the substitution is exact:
F = m a_c = m v^2/r. The lesson item `F = m\,a_c = \dfrac{m\,v^2}{r}` is this
substitution, and the s3 narration frames it correctly as "an acceleration needs
a force, Newton's second law names the cause" — i.e. the force identity F=ma
applied to a_c, with real agents named (friction, tension, gravity) matching the
standard treatment. Verdict: ok.

## Problem `s4` — centripetal acceleration of a cornering car, v = 20 m/s, r = 50 m

```python
v = 20; r = 50
a_c = v**2 / r
omega = v / r
print('a_c = 20^2/50 =', a_c, 'm/s^2')
print('check expr 20**2 / 50 =', 20**2 / 50, '(boxed 8)')
print('a_c / 9.8 =', round(a_c/9.8, 4), 'g')
print('omega = v/r =', omega, 'rad/s')
print('r*omega^2 =', r*omega**2, 'm/s^2 (equals v^2/r)')
```

output:

```
a_c = 20^2/50 = 8.0 m/s^2
check expr 20**2 / 50 = 8.0 (boxed 8)
a_c / 9.8 = 0.8163 g
omega = v/r = 0.4 rad/s
r*omega^2 = 8.000000000000002 m/s^2 (equals v^2/r)
```

a_c = 8.0 m/s^2 matches the boxed `answer`; it is about 0.816 g (s4 "four-fifths
of gravity"); and r ω^2 with ω = v/r = 0.4 rad/s also gives 8.0, confirming the
`omegaEq` cross-check. The `check.value` 8 agrees with the expr (tol 1 %).
Verdict: ok.
