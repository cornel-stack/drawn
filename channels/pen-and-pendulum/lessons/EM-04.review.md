## Equation `pointEq` — V = kQ/r (potential of a point charge)

§7.3 "ELECTRIC POTENTIAL V OF A POINT CHARGE", Eq **7.8** (p.285), quoted:

> "The electric potential V of a point charge is given by [V = kQ/r] where k is a
> constant equal to [8.99×10⁹ N·m²/C²]."

Reinforced by §7.5 (p.299), where the same formula explains why equipotentials
are spheres:

> "the potential for a point charge is given by [V = kQ/r] and thus has the same
> value at any point that is a given distance r from the charge."

And the 1/r fall-off claimed in the narration, §7.3 (p.285):

> "V for a point charge decreases with distance, whereas [E] for a point charge
> decreases with distance squared."

So V = kQ/r, falling as 1/r. The sign behaviour the narration describes (positive
charge builds a hill, negative digs a valley) is confirmed by §7.3 Example 7.10
(p.286): "The negative value for voltage means a positive charge would be
attracted from a larger distance, since the potential is lower (more negative)."
Board legend units V = J/C, Q in C, r in m are the standard SI units stated in
§7.2 p.275. ✔

## Equation `peEq` — U = qV (potential energy of a charge at a point)

§7.1 "Electric Potential Energy" (p.269), on the quantity and its units:

> "We use the letter U to denote electric potential energy, which has units of
> joules (J)."

That U depends on the specific charge and changes when it moves (narration: "the
energy a specific charge q has because of where it sits in the field … Move q and
this energy changes") — §7.1 p.268:

> "The work done by the applied force … on the charge Q changes the potential
> energy of Q. We call this potential energy the electrical potential energy of
> Q."

U = qV is the inverse of the potential definition V = U/q (§7.2 Eq 7.4, below)
and the same relation used in §7.2 Example 7.4: "the charge is given a change in
potential energy equal to [ΔU = qΔV]" (p.274). For a charge at a point of
potential V (reference zero at infinity), U = qV. The gravitational analogy
("just as lifting a mass changes its gravitational energy") is OpenStax's own
framing, §7.1 p.269: "Gravitational potential energy and electric potential
energy are quite analogous." ✔

## Equation `vEq` — V = U/q, in volts = J/C (potential is energy per unit charge)

§7.2 "Electric Potential", Eq **7.3** (p.274), the definition, quoted:

> "we define electric potential V (or simply potential, since electric is
> understood) to be the potential energy per unit charge:"

and the boxed statement Eq **7.4** (p.275):

> "ELECTRIC POTENTIAL — The electric potential energy per unit charge is [V =
> U/q]."

Independence of the test charge (narration: "what remains no longer depends on
the test charge … the property of the field"), §7.2 p.274:

> "Since U is proportional to q, the dependence on q cancels. Thus, V does not
> depend on q."

Units volt = joule/coulomb, §7.2 p.275:

> "Units of potential difference are joules per coulomb, given the name volt (V)
> after Alessandro Volta."

So V = U/q, measured in volts, one volt = one joule per coulomb, independent of
the test charge. ✔

## Equation `voltEq` — ΔV = V_b − V_a, W = qΔV (voltage is a potential difference)

§7.2 "Electric Potential Difference" (p.274), definition of ΔV between two points:

> "The change in potential energy ΔU is crucial, so we are concerned with the
> difference in potential or potential difference ΔV between two points, where
> [ΔV = V_B − V_A]."

Voltage = potential difference (narration: "voltage is nothing more than a
difference in potential between two points"), §7.2 p.274-275:

> "The familiar term voltage is the common name for electric potential
> difference. Keep in mind that whenever a voltage is quoted, it is understood to
> be the potential difference between two points. For example, every battery has
> two terminals, and its voltage is the potential difference between them."

The work relation W = qΔV, §7.2 "POTENTIAL DIFFERENCE AND ELECTRICAL POTENTIAL
ENERGY", Eq **7.5** (p.275):

> "The relationship between potential difference (or voltage) and electrical
> potential energy is given by [ΔU = qΔV]."

(W is the work done = the change in potential energy, ΔU = qΔV; the lesson writes
it as W = qΔV.) The "only differences matter / arbitrary zero" point (narration:
"The ground floor can be called zero wherever it is convenient"), §7.2 p.274:

> "the point you choose to be zero volts is arbitrary. This is analogous to the
> fact that gravitational potential energy has an arbitrary zero, such as sea
> level or perhaps a lecture hall floor."

And the 9-V battery example (narration: "A battery labelled nine volts guarantees
that difference between its terminals") matches §7.2's own 12-V battery framing
(Example 7.4, p.274). ✔

## Equipotential surfaces (narration in s2) — perpendicular to the field, constant potential

§7.5 "Equipotential Surfaces and Conductors" (p.299), quoted:

> "we use black lines to represent places where the electric potential is
> constant. These are called equipotential surfaces in three dimensions, or
> equipotential lines in two dimensions … Because the electric field lines point
> radially away from the charge, they are perpendicular to the equipotential
> lines."

and (p.300):

> "It is important to note that equipotential lines are always perpendicular to
> electric field lines. No work is required to move a charge along an
> equipotential … In other words, motion along an equipotential is perpendicular
> to E."

The rings closely packed where the field is strong — §7.5 topographic analogy
(p.302): "in regions of constant slope, the isolines would be evenly spaced"
(lines close together = steep = strong field, Fig 7.36 caption). The narration's
claim that the rings exist whether or not a charge sits on them (potential a
property of the field alone) is the §7.2 independence result ("V does not depend
on q", p.274) made pictorial by §7.5. ✔

## The field-is-independent-of-test-charge framing (narration, s2/s3)

§7.2 opening (p.274):

> "we defined electric field to be a quantity independent of the test charge in a
> given system … it is convenient to define a quantity that allows us to
> calculate the work on a charge independent of the magnitude of the charge."

Potential V is that quantity; it, like E, is a property of the source
configuration, not of the test charge. ✔

## Water-pressure analogy and the electron-volt (narration, s3)

The water / pressure analogy is a standard pedagogical device, not drawn from a
specific source line — flagged here as **standard** (pressure ∝ potential, flow
driven by pressure difference ∝ voltage). It introduces no quantitative claim.

The electron-volt definition **is** in the source, §7.2 "ELECTRON-VOLT" (p.278),
quoted:

> "it is more convenient to define an energy unit called the electron-volt (eV),
> which is the energy given to a fundamental charge accelerated through a
> potential difference of 1 V."

and p.277:

> "An electron accelerated through a potential difference of 1 V is given an
> energy of 1 eV."

This matches the narration ("move one electron through one volt and it gains one
electron-volt of energy — energy equals charge times potential difference"),
which is exactly W = qΔV written for q = e, ΔV = 1 V. ✔

## Units

U = qV: C·(J/C) = J. ✔ V = U/q: J/C = volt. ✔ ΔV = V_b − V_a: volt − volt =
volt. ✔ W = qΔV: C·(J/C) = J. ✔ V = kQ/r: (N·m²/C²)·C/m = N·m/C = J/C = volt. ✔
Dimensionally consistent throughout.
