## Equation `currentEq` — I = Q/t (definition of current)

§9.1 "Electrical Current", Eq **9.1/9.2** (p.362), quoted:

> "The average electrical current I is the rate at which charge flows, where
> [Q] is the amount of net charge passing through a given cross-sectional area in
> time [t] (Figure 9.2)."

(the displayed I = Q/t equation sat on the blank line after "the rate at which
charge flows,"). The unit, §9.1 p.362:

> "The SI unit for current is the ampere (A) … Since [I = Q/t], we see that an
> ampere is defined as one coulomb of charge passing through a given area per
> second"

so A = C/s, matching the board legend. The instantaneous form (narration
"defined as the rate at which charge flows past a point") is also stated, p.362:
"The instantaneous electrical current … is the time derivative of the charge that
flows" (Eq 9.3, I = dQ/dt). The board uses the average form I = Q/t, which is
Eq 9.1. ✔

**Conventional current direction** (narration: "it points opposite to the
electron drift, the way positive charge would move"), §9.1 p.365:

> "The direction of conventional current is always represented in the direction
> that positive charge would flow, from the positive terminal to the negative
> terminal … In metal wires, for example, current is carried by electrons—that is,
> negative charges move."

and §9.2 p.366: "Conventional current is in the direction opposite to the
movement of negative charge." The narration's historical note ("That convention
was fixed before anyone knew the carriers were negative") is OpenStax's own, §9.2
p.366: the convention "can be traced back to … Benjamin Franklin in the 1700s …
Having no knowledge of the particles that make up the atom". ✔

## Equation `microEq` — I = n q A v_d (current from drift velocity)

§9.2 "Model of Conduction in Metals", Eq **9.4** (p.368), quoted:

> "Rearranging terms gives [I = nqAv_d] where [v_d] is the drift velocity, n is
> the free charge density, A is the cross-sectional area of the wire, and I is
> the current through the wire. The carriers of the current each have charge q
> and move with a drift velocity of magnitude [v_d]."

(the displayed I = nqAv_d equation sat on the blank line after "Rearranging terms
gives"). The narration's reading — "number of electrons per unit volume, times
their charge, times the wire's cross-sectional area, times the drift velocity" —
is this line restated. **Drift velocity is small**, §9.2 p.367:

> "The drift velocity [v_d] is the average velocity of the free charges. Drift
> velocity is quite small, since there are so many free charges."

and the signal/drift reconciliation (narration: "the field itself travels …
at nearly the speed of light"), §9.2 p.366-367: signals "travel at speeds on the
order of [10⁸ m/s], a significant fraction of the speed of light," while "the
individual charges … move much slower on average," because "the electrons
behave as an incompressible fluid … as one charge enters, another leaves almost
immediately." The marble-tube analogy is our own restatement of this. ✔

## Equation `ohmEq` — V = IR (Ohm's law)

§9.4 "Ohm's Law", Eq **9.11** (p.382), quoted:

> "The slope of the line is the resistance, or the voltage divided by the
> current. This result is known as Ohm's law: [V = IR] where V is the voltage
> measured in volts across the object in question, I is the current measured
> through the object in amps, and R is the resistance in units of ohms."

(the displayed V = IR sat on the blank line after "This result is known as Ohm's
law:"). The direct proportionality, §9.4 p.380: "the current in a metal wire is
directly proportional to the voltage applied". ✔

**Ohm's law is NOT a fundamental law** (narration: "It is not a law of nature
like conservation of energy; it is a property that some materials happen to
have"), §9.4 p.380, quoted:

> "Many other materials do not show this relationship, so despite being called
> Ohm's law, it is not considered a law of nature, like Newton's laws or the laws
> of thermodynamics."

and p.381: "This is an empirical law, which is to say that it is an
experimentally observed phenomenon, like friction." **Ohmic = resistance
independent of V** (narration: "their resistance stays constant whatever the
voltage"), §9.4 p.380-381:

> "Any material, component, or device that obeys Ohm's law, where the current
> through the device is proportional to the voltage applied, is known as an ohmic
> material or ohmic component. Any material or component that does not obey Ohm's
> law is known as a nonohmic material or nonohmic component."

The narration's non-ohmic examples — "a filament bulb, a diode, a thermistor" —
are all in the source: the diode (§9.4 p.383, "the diode is an example of a
nonohmic device"), the temperature-dependent tungsten filament (§9.3 Example 9.6
p.378, "the current through the filament depends on the resistance of the
filament") and the thermistor (§9.3 p.378, "a semiconductor crystal with a strong
temperature dependence"). ✔

## Equation `resEq` — R = ρL/A (resistance from resistivity)

§9.3 "Resistivity and Resistance", Eq **9.9** (p.375), quoted:

> "The resistance of a cylindrical segment of a conductor is equal to the
> resistivity of the material times the length divided by the area"

(the displayed R = ρL/A sat on the blank line that follows), confirmed again at
p.376: "The resistance of the resistor is [R = ρL/A]." The definition R = V/I is
Eq 9.8, p.375: "The ratio of the voltage to the current is defined as the
resistance R." The geometry claims (narration: "A long thin wire of a poor
conductor resists strongly; a short fat wire of copper barely at all"), §9.3
Figure 9.15 p.376: "The longer the cylinder, the greater its resistance. The
larger its cross-sectional area A, the smaller its resistance." The
collisions-warm-the-conductor remark (narration: "The electrons lose energy
colliding with the vibrating atoms … that lost energy appears as heat"), §9.2
p.367: "they collide with the atoms in the lattice and with other electrons,
generating thermal energy, and the conductor gets warmer." ✔

## Worked problem `s4` — current through a resistor (equations `given`, `step1`, `answer`)

Self-contained: a resistor R = 4 Ω across V = 12 V; a direct application of Ohm's
law rearranged to I = V/R (§9.4 Eq 9.11; exactly the Example 9.8(b) move "The
current can be found using Ohm's law in the form I = V/R"). Numbers are our own —
Example 9.8 uses 9.00 V across the resistor and a measured current of 3.00 mA, a
different problem. Recomputation:

```python
V = 12.0   # volts
R = 4.0    # ohms
I = V / R
print("I = V/R =", I, "A")
```

Output:

```
I = V/R = 3.0 A
```

Board `step1` substitutes 12/4; `answer` boxes **3 A** — matches to 0.00 %. The
narration's "double the voltage and the current doubles" is immediate from
I = V/R at fixed R (the defining proportionality of an ohmic resistor). ✔

## Worked problem `s5` — power dissipated (equations `given2`, `step2`, `answer2`)

Self-contained: the same resistor and current (V = 12 V, I = 3 A); a direct
application of P = IV (§9.5 "Electrical Energy and Power": "The power supplied
from the battery is equal to current times the voltage, [P = IV]", p.385), with
the three equivalent forms P = IV = I²R = V²/R ("three different expressions for
electric power", §9.5 p.385). Numbers are our own — Example 9.9 uses 115 V and
20.00 A. Recomputation:

```python
V = 12.0   # volts
I = 3.0    # amps
R = 4.0    # ohms
P1 = V * I         # P = VI
P2 = I**2 * R      # P = I^2 R
P3 = V**2 / R      # P = V^2 / R
print("P = VI    =", P1, "W")
print("P = I^2 R =", P2, "W")
print("P = V^2/R =", P3, "W")
```

Output:

```
P = VI    = 36.0 W
P = I^2 R = 36.0 W
P = V^2/R = 36.0 W
```

All three forms give **36 W** (12·3 = 36; 3²·4 = 36; 12²/4 = 144/4 = 36). Board
`step2` substitutes (12)(3); `answer2` boxes **36 W** — matches to 0.00 %, as the
narration states ("all three return the same thirty-six watts"). The
energy-to-heat reading (narration: "the rate at which the resistor converts
electrical energy into heat") is §9.5 p.385: "With a resistor, the voltage drop
across the resistor is dissipated as heat." ✔

## Units

I = Q/t: C/s = ampere. ✔ A = C/s (stated §9.1). ✔ I = nqAv_d: (m⁻³)(C)(m²)(m/s)
= C/s = A. ✔ V = IR: A·Ω = A·(V/A) = V. ✔ R = ρL/A: (Ω·m)(m)/(m²) = Ω. ✔
P = VI: V·A = (J/C)(C/s) = J/s = W. ✔ I²R: A²·Ω = A²·(V/A) = A·V = W. ✔ V²/R:
V²/(V/A) = V·A = W. ✔ Dimensionally consistent throughout.
