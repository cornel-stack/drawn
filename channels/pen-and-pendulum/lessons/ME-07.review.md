# Fact-check — ME-07

All source quotations are from *University Physics Volume 1* (OpenStax, CC BY
4.0), Chapter 5 "Newton's Laws of Motion". Section files:
`039-5-2-newton-s-first-law.md`, `040-5-3-newton-s-second-law.md`,
`042-5-5-newton-s-third-law.md`.

## Equation `secondEq`

Lesson latex: `\vec{F}_{net} = m\vec{a}`.

> "NEWTON'S SECOND LAW OF MOTION — The acceleration of a system is directly
> proportional to and in the same direction as the net external force acting on
> the system and is inversely proportional to its mass. In equation form,
> Newton's second law is ... This is often written in the more familiar form"
> — §5.3, NEWTON'S SECOND LAW OF MOTION box, p.204.

The lesson's narration claim ("force does not cause motion; it causes a change
in motion") is directly supported by §5.3: "Newton's first law says that a net
external force causes a change in motion; thus, we see that a net external force
causes nonzero acceleration." The "mass = resistance to acceleration" legend
matches §5.2: "inertia, the ability of an object to resist changes in its
motion—in other words, to resist acceleration." Verdict: ok.

## Equation `firstEq`

Lesson latex: `\vec{F}_{net} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \vec{v} = \text{const}`.

> "the first law says that the velocity of an object remains constant if the net
> force on it is zero." — §5.2, Inertial Reference Frames, p.212.

Supporting statement box:

> "NEWTON'S FIRST LAW OF MOTION — A body at rest remains at rest or, if in
> motion, remains in motion at constant velocity unless acted on by a net
> external force." — §5.2, p.211.

Confirms the conceptual point that force changes velocity, not maintains it:
"Force is needed only to change velocity, not to maintain it" is backed by "the
object would not slow down if friction were eliminated." Verdict: ok.

## Equation `secondEq2`

Lesson latex: `\vec{F}_{net} = m\vec{a}` (same as `secondEq`, restated in the

> "In equation form, Newton's second law is ... This is often written in the
> more familiar form" (F_net = ma) — §5.3, NEWTON'S SECOND LAW OF MOTION box,
> p.204. Verdict: ok.

## Equation `thirdEq`

Lesson latex: `\vec{F}_{AB} = -\vec{F}_{BA}`.

> "NEWTON'S THIRD LAW OF MOTION — Whenever one body exerts a force on a second
> body, the first body experiences a force that is equal in magnitude and
> opposite in direction to the force that it exerts. Mathematically, if a body A
> exerts a force on body B, then B simultaneously exerts a force [the negative]
> on A, or in vector equation form," (Eq 5.10) — §5.5, p.214.

The lesson's key claim — the pair acts on **different bodies** and does **not
cancel** — is stated twice in the source:

> "You might think that two equal and opposite forces would cancel, but they do
> not because they act on different systems." — §5.5, p.214.

> "There are two important features of Newton's third law. First, the forces
> exerted (the action and reaction) are always equal in magnitude but opposite
> in direction. Second, these forces are acting on different bodies or systems:
> A's force on B ..." — §5.5, p.215.

Walking ("you push the ground backward, and the ground pushes you forward") and
the rocket example are both in the source (professor/floor and "Rockets move
forward by expelling gas backward at high velocity"). Verdict: ok.

## Problem `s5`

```python

m = 4            # kg

a = F_net / m

```

output: `12 N; 3.0 m/s^2` — matches boxed `answer2` (a = 3 m/s^2) and
`check.expr` `(30 - 18) / 4 = 3`. Verdict: ok.
