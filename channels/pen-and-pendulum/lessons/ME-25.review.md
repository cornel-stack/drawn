# Fact-check — ME-25 (Gyroscopic precession)

Source: OpenStax *University Physics Volume 1* (CC BY 4.0), §11.4 *Precession of a
Gyroscope* (PDF pp. 549–553) and §11.2 *Angular Momentum* (pp. 543–548).
Extracted text: `sources/text/university-physics-vol1/094-11-4-precession-of-a-gyroscope.md`.

> Note on quoting: the OpenStax PDF renders its displayed equations as images, so
> the equation glyphs are not in the extracted text — the surrounding prose that
> introduces each one is. Where that is so, the quoted line is the sentence that
> labels the equation, and the symbolic form is confirmed against the standard
> OpenStax equation number **and** against the worked example below, which this
> lesson's formula reproduces exactly.

## Equations

### `Leq` — L = I ω  (angular momentum of a rigid body)
§11.2, "Angular Momentum of a Rigid Body" (p.544):
> "anything that rotates about an axis carries angular momentum … the magnitude
> of the angular momentum of this mass segment is …"
Summed over the body this is the standard L = Iω for rotation about a fixed
symmetry axis, with **L** along the spin axis by the right-hand rule. ✔

### `tauLaw` — τ = dL/dt  (rotational form of Newton's second law)
§11.2 (p.546):
> "the net torque … we can write [τ = dL/dt]"
The net torque equals the time rate of change of the angular momentum vector. ✔

### `tauMag` — τ = r M g  (torque of the weight about the pivot)
§11.4 (p.551):
> "From Figure 11.21, we see that the magnitude of the torque is …"
With the axle horizontal the weight Mg acts at the centre of mass a distance r
from the pivot, perpendicular to the lever arm, so τ = rMg. ✔

### `dLeq` — dL = τ dt ⟂ L
§11.4 (p.551): the torque is horizontal and perpendicular to **L**, so the
increment dL = τ dt it produces is also perpendicular to **L** — it rotates **L**
without changing its magnitude. ✔

### `dphi` — dφ = dL / L
§11.4 (p.551), quoted verbatim:
> "The angle the top precesses through in time dt is [dφ = dL/L]" ✔

### `Omega` — Ω = dφ/dt = τ/L = r M g / (I ω)
§11.4 (p.551), Eq **11.12**, quoted verbatim:
> "The precession angular velocity is [Ω = rMg/(Iω)] and from this equation we
> see that …"
Confirmed numerically by Example 11.10 below (reproduces 3.12 rad/s). ✔

### `Teq` — T = 2π/Ω = 2π I ω / (r M g)
The precession sweeps the axle around a horizontal circle at constant rate Ω, so
one full turn takes T = 2π/Ω; substituting Eq 11.12 gives 2πIω/(rMg). ✔

## Worked problem `s6` (equations `given`/`step1`/`step2`/`step3`/`answer`)

Adapted from OpenStax **Example 11.10 "Period of Precession"** (p.552) with the
numbers changed (there: a disk, 0.3 kg, 20 rev/s, r = 5 cm; here: a wheel
modelled as a hoop, 2.0 kg, 12 rev/s, r = 12 cm). `step1` uses the hoop moment
of inertia I = M R² (§10.5, Table 10.4); `step2` uses ω = 2πf (§10.1).

Recomputation (Python, g = 9.8 m/s²):

```python
import math
M, R, f, r, g = 2.0, 0.33, 12.0, 0.12, 9.8
I  = M * R**2                 # hoop
w  = 2 * math.pi * f
Om = r * M * g / (I * w)      # Eq 11.12
T  = 2 * math.pi / Om
print(I, w, Om, T)
```

Output:

```
I  = 0.2178 kg m^2     (board: 0.22)   ✔
w  = 75.398 rad/s      (board: 75)     ✔
Om = 0.14322 rad/s     (board: 0.14)   ✔
T  = 43.869 s          (board: ~44)    ✔
```

**Validation of the formula against the source's own example** (disk, I = ½MR²):

```python
m, f2, r2, R2 = 0.3, 20.0, 0.05, 0.05
I2 = 0.5 * m * R2**2
Om2 = r2 * m * g / (I2 * 2*math.pi*f2)
print(round(Om2, 2))   # -> 3.12 rad/s  — matches OpenStax Example 11.10 exactly
```

## Units (pint)

Ω = r M g / (I ω): (m)(kg)(m/s²) / ((kg·m²)(rad/s)) = (kg·m²/s²) / (kg·m²/s) =
1/s = rad/s. T = 2π/Ω → s. Dimensionally consistent. ✔
