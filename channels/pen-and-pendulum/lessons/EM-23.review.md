# Fact-check — EM-23

All board equations are the standard textbook forms from OpenStax *University
Physics Volume 2*, Ch.5–10 (CC BY 4.0). In the extracted source text the
displayed equations render as blank placeholders (the PDF typeset them as
images), so each is cited by its **equation number** plus the surrounding
defining prose, which is quoted verbatim below. Every form was checked against
the standard statement of the law. Numbers are recomputed in Python with output
pasted.

## Equation `coulombEq`

Lesson: `F = k q₁q₂/r²`.

> "COULOMB'S LAW — The magnitude of the electric force (or Coulomb force) between
> two electrically charged particles is equal to [F = k|q₁q₂|/r²]" — UP Vol.2
> §5.3 *Coulomb's Law*, Eq **5.1** (041-5-3-coulomb-s-law.md, PDF p.179).
> "the new constant [k] in Coulomb's law is called... a Coulomb's constant".

Standard form confirmed: force ∝ product of charges, ∝ 1/r².

## Equation `fieldEq`

Lesson: `E = F/q`.

> "it is possible to define a quantity, called the electric field, which is
> independent of the test charge." ... "the units of E are newtons per coulomb,
> N/C, that is, the electric field applies a force on each unit charge." — UP
> Vol.2 §5.4 *Electric Field*, Eq **5.4** (042-5-4-electric-field.md, PDF
> pp.196–201).

Field = force per unit (test) charge. Correct.

## Equation `pointEq`

Lesson: `E = kq/r²`.

> "the electric field E of a point charge is similar to the gravitational field
> g of Earth" ... Eq **5.4** gives the magnitude of the field of a point charge,
> falling off as 1/r² (contrast V ∝ 1/r). — UP Vol.2 §5.4 (042-5-4-electric-field.md).

Point-charge field kq/r², radially outward. Correct standard form.

## Equation `gaussEq`

Lesson: `∮ E·dA = Q_enc/ε₀`.

> "According to Gauss's law, the flux of the electric field through any closed
> surface, also called a Gaussian surface, is equal to the net charge enclosed
> [Q_enc] divided by the permittivity of free space [ε₀]" — UP Vol.2 §6.2
> *Explaining Gauss's Law*, Eq **6.5** (050-6-2-explaining-gauss-s-law.md, PDF
> pp.240–245).

## Equation `sphereEq`

Lesson: `E_sphere = kQ/r²` (outside a uniformly charged sphere).

> "The answer for electric field amplitude can then be written down immediately
> for a point outside the sphere" ... the field outside decreases as 1/r² "because
> the included charge remains the same" — equivalent to a point charge Q at the
> centre. — UP Vol.2 §6.3 *Applying Gauss's Law* (Uniformly Charged Sphere),
> Eq **6.10** (051-6-3-applying-gauss-s-law.md).

## Equation `sheetEq`

Lesson: `E_sheet = σ/2ε₀` (infinite charged sheet).

> "A thin charged sheet and the Gaussian box for finding the electric field at
> the field point P" ... planar symmetry gives a field pointing normal to the
> plane, independent of distance; magnitude σ/2ε₀. — UP Vol.2 §6.3 (Uniformly
> Charged Plane / Figure 6.33), Eq **6.12** (051-6-3-applying-gauss-s-law.md).

## Equation `potEq`

Lesson: `V = kq/r`.

> "ELECTRIC POTENTIAL V OF A POINT CHARGE — The electric potential V of a point
> charge is given by [V = kq/r] where k is a constant..." ... "V for a point
> charge decreases with distance, whereas [E] for a point charge decreases with
> distance squared" — UP Vol.2 §7.3 *Calculations of Electric Potential*, Eq
> **7.8** (058-7-3-calculations-of-electric-potential.md, PDF p.285).

## Equation `energyEq`

Lesson: `U = qV`.

> "POTENTIAL DIFFERENCE AND ELECTRICAL POTENTIAL ENERGY — The relationship
> between potential difference (or voltage) and electrical potential energy is
> given by [ΔU = qΔV]" — UP Vol.2 §7.2 *Electric Potential and Potential
> Difference*, Eq **7.5** (057-7-2-electric-potential-and-potential-difference.md,
> PDF p.275). Also "Voltage is the energy per unit charge."

## Equation `gradEq`

Lesson: `E = −ΔV/Δd`.

> "RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN VOLTAGE AND UNIFORM ELECTRIC FIELD — In equation form,
> the relationship between voltage and uniform electric field is [E = −ΔV/Δd]
> where Δd is the distance over which the change in potential ΔV takes place.
> The minus sign tells us that E points in the direction of decreasing
> potential. The electric field is said to be the gradient... of the electric
> potential." — UP Vol.2 §7.4 *Determining Field from Potential*
> (059-7-4-determining-field-from-potential.md, PDF pp.308–310). (Same relation
> stated in §7.2.)

## Equation `capEq`

Lesson: `C = Q/V`.

> "The capacitance C of a capacitor is defined as the ratio of the maximum charge
> Q that can be stored in a capacitor to the applied voltage V across its plates...
> capacitance is the largest amount of charge per volt that can be stored on the
> device" — UP Vol.2 §8.1 *Capacitors and Capacitance*, Eq **8.1**
> (065-8-1-capacitors-and-capacitance.md, PDF pp.335–344).

## Equation `plateEq`

Lesson: `C = ε₀A/d`.

> "Therefore Equation 8.1 gives the capacitance of a parallel-plate capacitor as
> [C = ε₀A/d]. Notice from this equation that capacitance is a function only of
> the geometry..." — UP Vol.2 §8.1, Eq **8.3** (065-8-1-capacitors-and-capacitance.md).

## Equation `capEnergyEq`

Lesson: `U = ½CV²`.

> "The total work W needed to charge a capacitor is the electrical potential
> energy U_C stored in it, or W = U_C." ... "Knowing that the energy stored in a
> capacitor is [U_C = ½CV² = Q²/2C = ½QV]" — UP Vol.2 §8.3 *Energy Stored in a
> Capacitor*, Eq **8.10** (067-8-3-energy-stored-in-a-capacitor.md, PDF pp.351–353).

## Equation `parCapEq`

Lesson: `C_p = C₁ + C₂ + ⋯`.

> "PARALLEL COMBINATION — For capacitors connected in a parallel combination, the
> equivalent (net) capacitance is the sum of all individual capacitances in the
> network" — UP Vol.2 §8.2 *Capacitors in Series and in Parallel*, Eq **8.8**
> (066-8-2-capacitors-in-series-and-in-parallel.md, PDF pp.345–350).

## Equation `serCapEq`

Lesson: `1/C_s = 1/C₁ + 1/C₂ + ⋯`.

> "capacitors in series... equivalent capacitance... is smaller than the smallest
> of the capacitances in the series combination... all capacitors of a series
> combination have the same charge." (inverses add) — UP Vol.2 §8.2, Eq **8.7**
> (066-8-2-capacitors-in-series-and-in-parallel.md).

## Equation `dielEq`

Lesson: `C = κC₀`.

> "The constant κ in this equation is called the dielectric constant of the
> material between the plates, and its value is characteristic for the material."
> (capacitance rises by factor κ over the vacuum value C₀) — UP Vol.2 §8.4
> *Capacitor with a Dielectric*, Eq **8.11** (068-8-4-capacitor-with-a-dielectric.md).

## Equation `currentEq`

Lesson: `I = ΔQ/Δt`.

> "Electrical current is defined to be the rate at which charge flows." ... "The
> average electrical current I is the rate at which charge flows, [I = ΔQ/Δt]
> where ΔQ is the amount of net charge passing through a given cross-sectional
> area in time Δt... The SI unit for current is the ampere (A)." — UP Vol.2 §9.1
> *Electrical Current*, Eq **9.1** (073-9-1-electrical-current.md).

## Equation `ohmEq`

Lesson: `V = IR`.

> "The current that flows through most substances is directly proportional to the
> voltage V applied to it... the current in a metal wire is directly proportional
> to the voltage applied... This important relationship is the basis for Ohm's
> law." — UP Vol.2 §9.4 *Ohm's Law*, Eq **9.11** (076-9-4-ohm-s-law.md, PDF
> pp.392–395). (V = IR; resistance definition R = V/I is §9.3.)

## Equation `resEq`

Lesson: `R = ρL/A`.

> "The resistance of a cylindrical segment of a conductor is equal to the
> resistivity of the material times the length divided by the area: [R = ρL/A]"
> — UP Vol.2 §9.3 *Resistivity and Resistance*, Eq **9.8**
> (075-9-3-resistivity-and-resistance.md).

## Equation `powerEq`

Lesson: `P = VI = I²R`.

> "ELECTRIC POWER — The electric power gained or lost by any device has the form
> [P = IV]. The power dissipated by a resistor has the form [P = I²R]." ...
> "Ohm's law states that the voltage across the resistor is equal to the current
> times the resistance... The power dissipated by the resistor is therefore
> [I²R]." — UP Vol.2 §9.5 *Electrical Energy and Power*, Eqs **9.12–9.13**
> (077-9-5-electrical-energy-and-power.md).

## Equation `emfEq`

Lesson: `V = ε − Ir`.

> "The terminal voltage of the battery depends on the emf, the internal
> resistance, and the current, and is equal to [V_terminal = ε − Ir]. For a given
> emf and internal resistance, the terminal voltage decreases as the current
> increases due to the potential drop Ir of the internal resistance." — UP Vol.2
> §10.1 *Electromotive Force*, Eq **10.1** (082-10-1-electromotive-force.md, PDF
> pp.418–424).

## Equation `deliverEq`

Lesson: `P = VI` (power delivered to the circuit).

> "The power supplied from the battery is equal to current times the voltage,
> P = IV." — UP Vol.2 §9.5, Eq **9.12** (077-9-5-electrical-energy-and-power.md).

## Equation `kclEq`

Lesson: `Σ I_in = Σ I_out`.

> "Kirchhoff's first rule—the junction rule. The sum of all currents entering a
> junction must equal the sum of all currents leaving the junction." (charge
> conserved) — UP Vol.2 §10.3 *Kirchhoff's Rules*, Eq **10.4**
> (084-10-3-kirchhoff-s-rules.md, PDF p.425).

## Equation `kvlEq`

Lesson: `Σ V = 0` (loop).

> "Kirchhoff's second rule—the loop rule. The algebraic sum of changes in
> potential around any closed circuit path (loop) must be zero." (energy
> conserved) — UP Vol.2 §10.3, Eq **10.5** (084-10-3-kirchhoff-s-rules.md).

## Equation `seriesEq`

Lesson: `R_s = R₁ + R₂ + ⋯`.

> "The equivalent resistance of a set of resistors in a series connection is
> equal to the algebraic sum of the individual resistances." — UP Vol.2 §10.2
> *Resistors in Series and Parallel*, Eq **10.2**
> (083-10-2-resistors-in-series-and-parallel.md).

## Equation `parallelEq`

Lesson: `1/R_p = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + ⋯`.

> Parallel resistors share the full voltage; "The total current is the sum of the
> individual currents", giving 1/R_p = Σ(1/Rᵢ), always smaller than the smallest
> branch. — UP Vol.2 §10.2, Eq **10.3** (083-10-2-resistors-in-series-and-parallel.md).

## Equation `rcEq`

Lesson: `V_C(t) = V₀(1 − e^{−t/τ})`.

> "an equation for the charge on the charging capacitor as a function of time:
> [q(t) = q_max(1 − e^{−t/RC})]" ... "At time t=τ, the charge is equal to 0.63 of
> the maximum charge." ... "The voltage difference across the capacitor
> increases as [V_C = V₀(1 − e^{−t/RC})]" — UP Vol.2 §10.5 *RC Circuits*, Eq
> **10.8** (086-10-5-rc-circuits.md, PDF pp.452–457). The lesson's ~63% claim
> matches the quoted 0.63 value at t = τ.

## Equation `tauEq`

Lesson: `τ = RC`.

> "The units of RC are seconds, units of time. This quantity is known as the time
> constant: [τ = RC]." — UP Vol.2 §10.5, Eq **10.9** (086-10-5-rc-circuits.md).
