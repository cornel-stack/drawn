## Equation `capEq` — C = Q/V (definition of capacitance)

§8.1 "Capacitors and Capacitance", Eq **8.1** (p.325), quoted:

> "The capacitance C of a capacitor is defined as the ratio of the maximum
> charge Q that can be stored in a capacitor to the applied voltage V across its
> plates. In other words, capacitance is the largest amount of charge per volt
> that can be stored on the device:"

(the displayed C = Q/V equation, labelled 8.1, sat on the next line). The
narration's gloss — "the charge on either plate divided by the voltage between
them. More charge held for each volt means a larger capacitance" — is this line
restated. V is the potential difference, §8.1 p.325:

> "in Equation 8.1, V represents the potential difference between the capacitor
> plates, not the potential at any one point."

Board legend unit farad = C/V, §8.1 p.325:

> "The SI unit of capacitance is the farad (F) … Since capacitance is the charge
> per unit voltage, one farad is one coulomb per one volt."

So C = Q/V, measured in farads = coulombs per volt. ✔

## Equation `plateEq2` — C = ε₀A/d (parallel-plate capacitance)

§8.1 "Parallel-Plate Capacitor", Eq **8.4** (derived from the field Eq 8.2 and
V = Ed), p.327, quoted:

> "Therefore Equation 8.1 gives the capacitance of a parallel-plate capacitor as"

(the displayed C = ε₀A/d equation followed), and immediately:

> "Notice from this equation that capacitance is a function only of the geometry
> and what material fills the space between the plates (in this case, vacuum) of
> this capacitor."

ε₀ is the permittivity of free space, §8.1 p.327: "the constant ε₀ is the
permittivity of free space". The narration's qualitative claims — "bigger plates
and a smaller gap store more charge" — are OpenStax's own reasoning, §8.1 p.326:

> "the bigger the plates are, the more charge they can store. Thus, C should be
> greater for a larger value of A … the closer the plates are together … C
> should be greater for a smaller d."

So C = ε₀A/d: capacitance proportional to plate area A, inversely proportional to
gap d. ✔

## Equation `energyEq` — U = ½QV = ½CV² = ½Q²/C (energy stored), and the ½

§8.3 "Energy Stored in a Capacitor", the charging-integral derivation, p.340,
quoted:

> "To move an infinitesimal charge dq from the negative plate to the positive
> plate (from a lower to a higher potential), the amount of work dW that must be
> done on dq is"

(the displayed dW = (q/C)dq followed), then:

> "In order to charge the capacitor to a charge Q, the total work required is"

(the displayed integral giving W = Q²/(2C) = ½QV = ½CV², Eq **8.9/8.10**,
followed), and:

> "Because [Q = CV], we can express this result in other equivalent forms:"

(the ½QV = ½CV² = ½Q²/C trio followed). The factor-of-½ origin — the narration's
"the voltage is not constant as you charge up … the voltage climbs from zero to
its final value" — is exactly this integral: dW = (q/C)dq with q running 0→Q
gives ½, i.e. the average charging voltage is half the final voltage. The source
states the work is the stored energy, p.340:

> "The total work W needed to charge a capacitor is the electrical potential
> energy U stored in it, or [U = W]."

Energy in the field, §8.3 p.339-340:

> "A charged capacitor stores energy in the electrical field between its plates
> … When a charged capacitor is disconnected from a battery, its energy remains
> in the field in the space between its plates."

Energy density u = ½ε₀E², §8.3 Eq **8.9** (p.340):

> "the energy density u in a region of free space occupied by an electrical
> field E depends only on the magnitude of the field and is"

(the displayed u = ½ε₀E² followed; confirmed again at p.341, "divide U by the
volume Ad … we obtain" the same u = ½ε₀E²). This underwrites the narration's
"rewrite one half C V squared using the field in the gap, and it becomes one half
the permittivity times the field squared, summed over the volume". ✔

## Equation `dielEq` — C → κC₀ (dielectric multiplies capacitance)

§8.4 "Capacitor with a Dielectric", Eq **8.11** (p.343), quoted:

> "This equation tells us that the capacitance C₀ of an empty (vacuum) capacitor
> can be increased by a factor of κ when we insert a dielectric material to
> completely fill the space between its plates."

The voltage-drop mechanism (narration: "the bound charges weaken the field and
lower the voltage for the same stored charge"), §8.4 p.342:

> "we find that the voltmeter reading has dropped to a smaller value V. We write
> this new voltage value as a fraction of the original voltage V₀, with a
> positive number κ"

(displayed V = V₀/κ followed), and p.342:

> "the insertion of the dielectric has no effect on the charge on the plate,
> which remains at a value of Q₀."

Same charge Q₀ at lower voltage V₀/κ ⇒ C = Q₀/(V₀/κ) = κC₀. The induced (bound)
charges, §8.4 p.343:

> "there will be the induced charges on the surface of the sample … These
> induced charges on the dielectric surface are of an opposite sign to the free
> charges on the plates."

The narration's "dielectric of kappa equal to five turns any capacitor into one
five times better" is Eq 8.11 with κ = 5 (a representative value; the source's
Teflon example in Example 8.10 uses κ = 2.1, p.344 — our figure is illustrative,
not copied). ✔

## Units

C = Q/V: C/V = farad. ✔ C = ε₀A/d: (F/m)·m²/m = F. ✔ U = ½QV: C·(J/C) = J; ½CV²:
F·V² = (C/V)·V² = C·V = J; ½Q²/C: C²/(C/V) = C·V = J. ✔ u = ½ε₀E²: (F/m)·(V/m)² =
(C/V/m)·V²/m² = C·V/m³ = J/m³. ✔ C → κC: κ dimensionless, so F stays F. ✔
Dimensionally consistent throughout.
