## Equations

### `qne` — q = n·e, e = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C  (quantisation of charge)
§5.1 "Properties of Electric Charge" (p.173), quoted:
> "Charge is quantized. This means that electric charge comes in discrete
> amounts, and there is a smallest possible amount of charge that an object can
> have. In the SI system, this smallest amount is [e]. No free particle can have
> less charge than this, and, therefore, the charge on any object … must be an
> integer multiple of this amount."
And: "the smallest possible positive charge (to four significant figures) is
[1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C]". The value in the extracted text is an image; 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C is
the standard OpenStax four-significant-figure value. The integer-multiple
statement is exactly q = n·e. ✔

### `coulomb` — F = k |q₁q₂| / r²  (Coulomb's law)
§5.3 "COULOMB'S LAW", Eq **5.1** (p.179), introducing prose quoted:
> "The magnitude of the force is linearly proportional to the net charge on each
> object and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them …
> The magnitude of the electric force (or Coulomb force) between two electrically
> charged particles is equal to [Eq 5.1]."
The displayed equation (image, numbered 5.1) is F = k|q₁q₂|/r². The inverse-square
claim in the lesson narration matches §5.1 p.171: "the magnitude of the force
decreases as the square of the distance … when the distance … is doubled, the
force … decreases to one fourth". ✔

### `kval` — k = 1/(4πε₀) = 8.99×10⁹ N·m²/C²
§5.3 (p.179), quoted:
> "the new constant ε₀ in Coulomb's law is called the permittivity of free space
> … Its numerical value (to three significant figures) turns out to be [8.85×10⁻¹²]
> … For convenience, we often define a Coulomb's constant: [k = 1/(4πε₀)]."
Value check: 1/(4π·8.85×10⁻¹²) = 8.99×10⁹ N·m²/C². The ε₀ and k displayed values
are images; both are the standard OpenStax three-significant-figure values. ✔

### `superpos` — F_net = Σ F_i = F₁ + F₂ + ⋯  (superposition)
§5.3 "Multiple Source Charges", Eq **5.2** (p.181), quoted:
> "the net electric force on our test charge is simply the vector sum of each
> individual electric force exerted on it by each of the individual source
> charges … This ability to simply add up individual forces in this way is
> referred to as the principle of superposition … In mathematical form, this
> becomes [Eq 5.2]."
The displayed equation (image, numbered 5.2) is the vector sum F_net = Σ F_i. ✔

### `combine` — F_net = √(Fx² + Fy²), tanθ = Fy/Fx
§5.3 Example 5.2 (p.183), quoted:
> "We can't add these forces directly because they don't point in the same
> direction: [F₁] points only in the −x-direction, while [F₂] points only in the
> +y-direction. The net force is obtained from applying the Pythagorean theorem
> to its x- and y-components."
So magnitude = √(Fx²+Fy²) and direction by the tangent of the components. ✔
