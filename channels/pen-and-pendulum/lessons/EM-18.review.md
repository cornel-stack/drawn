## Equation `zEq`

`Z = √(R² + (X_L − X_C)²)` — the impedance.

> "Furthermore, from the Pythagorean theorem, [Eq. 15.11] ... where [Z] is
> known as the impedance of the circuit. Its unit is the ohm, and it is the
> ac analog to resistance in a dc circuit, which measures the combined effect
> of resistance, capacitive reactance, and inductive reactance."
> — §15.3 RLC Series Circuits with AC, Eq. 15.11, p.633

The legend entries `X_L` (inductive reactance, ωL, Ω) and `X_C` (capacitive
reactance, 1/ωC, Ω) are corroborated by §15.2 (Eq. 15.8 and Eq. 15.3), see
`xlEq`/`xcEq` below, and the stated unit (ohm) matches the source.

## Equation `xlEq`

`X_L = ωL` — inductive reactance, rising with frequency.

> "The quantity [X_L] is known as the inductive reactance of the inductor, or
> the opposition of an inductor to a change in current; its unit is also the
> ohm. Note that [X_L] varies directly as the frequency of the ac source—high
> frequency causes high inductive reactance."
> — §15.2 Simple AC Circuits, Eq. 15.8, p.629

The narration's claim "it rises with frequency" matches "varies directly as
the frequency ... high frequency causes high inductive reactance."

## Equation `xcEq`

`X_C = 1/(ωC)` — capacitive reactance, falling with frequency.

> "The quantity [X_C] is known as the capacitive reactance of the capacitor,
> or the opposition of a capacitor to a change in current. It depends
> inversely on the frequency of the ac source—high frequency leads to low
> capacitive reactance."
> — §15.2 Simple AC Circuits, Eq. 15.3, p.627

The narration's claim "it falls with frequency" matches "depends inversely on
the frequency ... high frequency leads to low capacitive reactance."

## Equation `zEq2`

Identical relation to `zEq`, restated at the end of the derivation.

> "Furthermore, from the Pythagorean theorem, [Eq. 15.11] ... known as the
> impedance of the circuit." — §15.3, Eq. 15.11, p.633

The derivation's route — resistor phasor along the current, reactance phasors
antiparallel at right angles, combine by Pythagoras — matches the source's
phasor construction:

> "the current (I) leads the emf (E) in a capacitor (C)" / "the emf (E) leads
> the current (I) in an inductor (L)" (ELI the ICE man); "If there is both a
> capacitor and an inductor, find the net voltage from these two phasors,
> since they are antiparallel." — §15.3, p.634

## Equation `ohmEq`

`I₀ = V₀/Z` — the ac version of Ohm's law.

> "The current amplitude is therefore the ac version of Ohm's law: [Eq. 15.10]
> where [Z] ... is known as the impedance of the circuit."
> — §15.3, Eq. 15.10, p.633

## Equation `resEq`

`X_L = X_C ⇒ ω₀ = 1/√(LC)` — the resonance condition and resonant frequency.

> "The resonant frequency [ω0] of the RLC circuit is the frequency at which
> the amplitude of the current is a maximum and the circuit would oscillate if
> not driven by a voltage source. By inspection, this corresponds to the
> angular frequency [ω0] at which the impedance Z in Equation 15.15 is a
> minimum, or when [X_L = X_C] and [ω0 = 1/√(LC)]. This is the resonant
> angular frequency of the circuit ... Therefore, at resonance, an RLC circuit
> is purely resistive, with the applied emf and current in phase."
> — §15.5 Resonance in an AC Circuit, Eq. 15.17, p.640-641

This is the standard result, identical to the free LC oscillation frequency
(cf. EM-17), as the narration states. The narration's further claims — net
reactance zero, impedance equal to R, phase shift vanishes — match "at
resonance, an RLC circuit is purely resistive, with the applied emf and
current in phase."
