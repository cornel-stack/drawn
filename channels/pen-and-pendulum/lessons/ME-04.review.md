# Fact-check — ME-04 (Projectile motion from zero: one method for every question)

Source: OpenStax *University Physics Volume 1* (CC BY 4.0), Chapter 4 *Motion in
Two and Three Dimensions*, §4.3 *Projectile Motion*. Extracted text:
`sources/text/university-physics-vol1/032-4-3-projectile-motion.md` (the header
marks it PDF pp. 172–181; the printed running-foot page numbers are 160–169,
which is what the lesson's `source.pages: 160-167` records and what is cited
below).

> Note on quoting: the OpenStax PDF renders its displayed equations as images, so
> the equation glyphs (Eqs 4.19–4.26, and the component/kinematic equations) are
> not present in the extracted text — only the surrounding prose and the equation
> numbers printed in the margin. Where that is so, the quoted line is the sentence
> that introduces or labels the equation, and the symbolic form is confirmed
> against the standard OpenStax equation number.

## Context confirmed from §4.3

- **Projectile motion = motion under gravity alone, air resistance ignored, path
  is a parabola** (p.160):
  > "Projectile motion is the motion of an object thrown or projected into the
  > air, subject only to acceleration as a result of gravity. … their path is
  > called a trajectory." And:
  > "In this section, we consider two-dimensional projectile motion, and our
  > treatment neglects the effects of air resistance." The parabola is confirmed
  > under 'Trajectory' (p.167):
  > "This trajectory equation is of the form … which is an equation of a parabola".
  This supports the s2 narration ("a parabola … moving under gravity alone, with
  air resistance ignored").
- **Horizontal and vertical motions are INDEPENDENT** (p.160):
  > "The most important fact to remember here is that motions along perpendicular
  > axes are independent and thus can be analyzed separately. … vertical and
  > horizontal motions are independent." This is the lesson's "surprising fact"
  > (s2) and the thumbnail hook "Up and across don't talk".
- **Horizontal has no acceleration (a_x = 0, constant velocity)** (p.160):
  > "there is no acceleration along the horizontal axis when air resistance is
  > negligible". And (Fig 4.12, p.161): "The horizontal motion is simple, because
  > [v_x] is a constant." This underpins `xEq` (x = x0 + v0x t).
- **Vertical is free fall (a_y = -g)** (p.160–161):
  > "Defining the positive direction to be upward, the components of acceleration
  > are then very simple: Because gravity is vertical". The vertical kinematic
  > equations are the free-fall set with a = -g — underpins `yEq`.
- **The one method: resolve, treat as two independent 1-D motions, solve each,
  recombine; time is the shared variable** (Problem-Solving Strategy, p.161):
  > "1. Resolve the motion into horizontal and vertical components along the x-
  > and y-axes. … 2. Treat the motion as two independent one-dimensional motions
  > … 3. Solve for the unknowns in the two separate motions … Note that the only
  > common variable between the motions is time t. … 4. Recombine quantities in
  > the horizontal and vertical directions". This is exactly the s3 narration
  > ("Resolve, solve each axis, recombine").

## Equation `xEq` — x = x0 + v0x t

§4.3 Horizontal Motion kinematic equations (p.161). With a_x = 0, the horizontal
position equation x = x0 + v0x t + 1/2 a_x t^2 reduces to x = x0 + v0x t, the
constant-velocity form. Confirmed by Fig 4.12 (p.161): "The horizontal motion is
simple, because [v_x] is a constant." The lesson item `x = x_0 + v_{0x}\,t`
matches. Verdict: ok.

## Equation `yEq` — y = y0 + v0y t - 1/2 g t^2

§4.3 Vertical Motion kinematic equations, **Equation 4.22** (p.161). Eq 4.22 is
the position equation specialised to a_y = -g: y = y0 + v0y t - 1/2 g t^2.
Confirmed by its use in Ex 4.8(a) (p.165): "We can find the time for this by
using Equation 4.22". The lesson item
`y = y_0 + v_{0y}\,t - \tfrac{1}{2}\,g\,t^2` matches. Verdict: ok.

## Equation `comps` — v0x = v0 cos theta, v0y = v0 sin theta

§4.3 Problem-Solving Strategy step 1, **Equations 4.19–4.20** (p.161):
> "The magnitudes of the components of velocity are [v_x = v cos theta, v_y = v
> sin theta] where v is the magnitude of the velocity and θ is its direction
> relative to the horizontal". The lesson item
`v_{0x} = v_0\cos\theta, \; v_{0y} = v_0\sin\theta` matches. Verdict: ok.

## Equation `tofStep` — 0 = v0y t - 1/2 g t^2 => t(v0y - 1/2 g t) = 0

§4.3 'Time of flight' (p.166):
> "We note the position and displacement in y must be zero at launch and at
> impact on an even surface. Thus, we set the displacement in y equal to zero and
> find [0 = v0y t - 1/2 g t^2]. Factoring, we have [t(v0y - 1/2 g t) = 0]."
The lesson item matches this factoring step. Verdict: ok.

## Equation `tof` — T = 2 v0 sin theta / g

§4.3 'Time of flight', **Equation 4.24** (p.166):
> "Solving for t gives us [T = 2 v0 sin theta / g]. This is the time of flight
> for a projectile both launched and impacting on a flat horizontal surface."
The lesson item `T = \dfrac{2 v_0 \sin\theta}{g}` matches. Verdict: ok.

## Equation `range` — R = v0^2 sin 2theta / g (max at 45 deg)

§4.3 'Range', **Equation 4.26** (p.167):
> "Using the trigonometric identity … and setting x = R for range, we find [R =
> v0^2 sin 2theta / g]. … we see from the factor [sin 2theta] that the range is
> maximum at [theta = 45 deg]."
The lesson item `R = \dfrac{v_0^2 \sin 2\theta}{g}`, with the s4 narration
"greatest when two theta is ninety — that is, a launch angle of forty-five
degrees", matches. Verdict: ok.

## Equation `maxh` — H = v0^2 sin^2 theta / (2g)

§4.3 Example 4.7 closing result (p.163):
> "Call the maximum height y = h. Then, [h = v0y^2 / (2g)]. This equation defines
> the maximum height of a projectile above its launch position and it depends
> only on the vertical component of the initial velocity."
With v0y = v0 sin theta this is H = v0^2 sin^2 theta / (2g). The lesson item
`H = \dfrac{v_0^2 \sin^2\theta}{2g}`, with the s4 narration "set only by the
vertical component", matches. Verdict: ok.

## Problem `s5` — launch at an angle, v0 = 28 m/s, theta = 30 deg

```python
import math
v0 = 28; th = math.radians(30); g = 9.8
v0x = v0 * math.cos(th); v0y = v0 * math.sin(th)
H = v0y**2 / (2*g); T = 2*v0y / g; R = v0**2 * math.sin(2*th) / g
print('v0x = 28 cos30 =', round(v0x, 4))
print('v0y = 28 sin30 =', round(v0y, 4))
print('H = 14^2/(2*9.8) =', round(H, 4))
print('T = 2*14/9.8 =', round(T, 4))
print('R = 28^2 sin60 / 9.8 =', round(R, 4))
print('check expr 28**2 * (3**0.5/2) / 9.8 =', 28**2 * (3**0.5/2) / 9.8)
```
