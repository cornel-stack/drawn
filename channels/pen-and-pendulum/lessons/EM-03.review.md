## Equations

### `fluxEq` — Φ = E·A = EA cosθ  (electric flux)
§6.1 "Electric Flux" subsection, Eq **6.1** (p.222), quoted:
> "Now that we have defined the area vector of a surface, we can define the
> electric flux of a uniform electric field through a flat area as the scalar
> product of the electric field and the area vector"

and the orientation dependence (p.221):
> "if the area is rotated so that the plane is aligned with the field lines, none
> will pass through and there will be no flux."

The displayed Eq 6.1 (image) is Φ = E·A = EA cosθ; the scalar-product definition
and the "edge-on ⇒ zero, head-on ⇒ maximum" behaviour are stated verbatim. The
lesson's legend units N·m²/C match p.221 ("SI unit of newton-meters squared per
coulomb"). The outward-normal sign convention (lines leaving = positive, entering
= negative) is confirmed p.222 ("when field lines leave … Φ is positive; when
they enter … Φ is negative"). ✔

### `gaussEq` — Φ = ∮E·dA = Q_enc/ε0  (Gauss's law)
§6.2 "Statement of Gauss's Law" and the boxed "GAUSS'S LAW", Eq **6.5**
(p.230–231), quoted:
> "According to Gauss's law, the flux of the electric field E through any closed
> surface, also called a Gaussian surface, is equal to the net charge enclosed
> Q_enc divided by the permittivity of free space ε0"

and the boxed statement (p.231):
> "The flux Φ of the electric field E through any closed surface S (a Gaussian
> surface) is equal to the net charge enclosed Q_enc divided by the permittivity
> of free space ε0"

The closed-surface integral ∮E·dA and the three emphasised properties are
sourced: any shape / imaginary boundary (p.230: "The Gaussian surface does not
need to correspond to a real, physical object … It is a mathematical construct
that may be of any shape, provided that it is closed"); only enclosed charge
counts (p.231: "Q_enc is just the charge inside the Gaussian surface"); exterior
charges net zero (p.222/§6.2 p.229: "Every line that enters the surface must also
leave that surface. Hence the net 'flow' … is zero"). ✔

### `sphereEq` — Φ = E(4πr²) = Q/ε0  (sphere)
§6.2 p.228 (flux of a point charge through a concentric sphere), quoted:
> "We now find the net flux by integrating this flux over the surface of the
> sphere … where the total surface area of the spherical surface is [4πr²]."

and §6.3 "Using Gauss's law", Eq **6.8** (p.236):
> "The magnitude of the electric field E must be the same everywhere on a
> spherical Gaussian surface concentric with the distribution."

By symmetry E is constant and radial over the sphere, so Φ = E·4πr²; setting this
equal to Q/ε0 is the Gauss's-law step on p.236. ✔

### `sphereRes` — E = Q/(4πε0 r²) = kQ/r²  (point charge / sphere field)
§6.3 Eq **6.8**, p.236 — solving E(4πr²) = Q/ε0 gives E = Q/(4πε0 r²) = kQ/r²
(since k = 1/(4πε0)). The "looks like a point charge from outside" claim is
§6.3 Significance (p.238):
> "Notice that [E_out] has the same form as the equation of the electric field of
> an isolated point charge. In determining the electric field of a uniform
> spherical charge distribution, we can therefore assume that all of the charge
> inside the appropriate spherical Gaussian surface is located at the center"

The inverse-square/area-growth cancellation intuition (narration s2) is §6.2
p.228:
> "the flux is independent of the size of the spherical surface. This can be
> directly attributed to the fact that the electric field of a point charge
> decreases as [1/r²] with distance, which just cancels the [r²] rate of increase
> of the surface area." ✔

### `lineEq` — Φ = E(2πrL) = λL/ε0  (cylinder)
§6.3 "Charge Distribution with Cylindrical Symmetry" / "Gaussian surface and flux
calculation" (p.242), quoted:
> "The electric field is perpendicular to the cylindrical side and parallel to the
> planar end caps of the surface. The flux through the cylindrical part is [E·2πrL]
> whereas the flux through the end caps is zero because E·n = 0 there."

and (p.242):
> "the flux must equal the amount of charge within the volume enclosed by this
> surface, divided by the permittivity of free space … Let us write it as charge
> per unit length λ times length L"

So Φ = E(2πrL) = λL/ε0; lengths cancel. (The source writes the radius as s;
the lesson's r is the same quantity.) ✔

### `lineRes` — E = λ/(2πε0 r)  (infinite line field)
§6.3 Example **6.8** "Uniformly Charged Cylindrical Shell" (p.243), quoted:
> "Outside the shell, the result becomes identical to a wire with uniform
> charge [λ]"

and Check 6.5 (p.243):
> "A thin straight wire has a uniform linear charge density λ. Find the electric
> field at a distance d from the wire"

Solving E(2πrL) = λL/ε0 gives E = λ/(2πε0 r) = 2kλ/r, falling off as 1/r (not
1/r²), as the lesson stresses. ✔

### `planeEq` — Φ = 2EA = σA/ε0  (box straddling a sheet)
§6.3 "Charge Distribution with Planar Symmetry" / "Gaussian surface and flux
calculation" (p.245), quoted:
> "sides I and II of the Gaussian surface (the box) that are parallel to the
> infinite plane have been shaded. They are the only surfaces that give rise to
> nonzero flux because the electric field and the area vectors of the other faces
> are perpendicular to each other."

and (p.245):
> "we find for the flux of electric field through the box [Φ = EA + EA = 2EA] …
> the charges inside the volume enclosed by the Gaussian box reside on an area A
> of the xy-plane. Hence [Q_enc = σA]."

So Φ = 2EA = σA/ε0. ✔

### `planeRes` — E = σ/(2ε0)  (infinite sheet field)
§6.3 planar symmetry, Eqs **6.11–6.12** (p.245), quoted:
> "Using the equations for the flux and enclosed charge in Gauss's law, we can
> immediately determine the electric field at a point at height z from a
> uniformly charged plane in the xy-plane"

Solving 2EA = σA/ε0 gives E = σ/(2ε0), independent of z (the narration's "it does
not depend on distance at all"). The factor of 2 is because the sheet throws
field out of both faces — the two shaded sides I and II of the box each carry EA.
The distance-independence matches p.244's statement that for planar symmetry the
field depends only on the direction from the plane (uniform in z). ✔

## Worked problem `s6` — infinite line of charge (`given`, `step1`, `answer`)

Self-contained: λ = 3.0 nC/m, r = 0.15 m; a direct application of the
infinite-line field E = λ/(2πε0 r) = 2kλ/r (§6.3 Example 6.8 / Check 6.5).
Numbers are our own — OpenStax Example 6.8 leaves the result symbolic and Check
6.5 uses the symbol λ with no numeric values, so no verbatim copy. Recomputation:

```python
k = 8.99e9; eps0 = 8.85e-12; pi = 3.141592653589793
E_expr = 2*8.99e9*3.0e-9/0.15          # board step: 2k lambda / r
E_full = 3.0e-9/(2*pi*8.85e-12*0.15)   # lambda / (2 pi eps0 r)
print(E_expr, E_full)
```

Output:

```
359.6 359.67218777829453
```

The two forms agree (359.6 vs 359.7 N/C — the tiny gap is because 2k = 1.798×10¹⁰
vs 1/(2πε0) = 1.7975×10¹⁰, a 0.02 % rounding of k). Board `answer` boxes
**≈ 360 N/C (away from the wire)**; the boxed `check.value` is 359.7. Direction
radially away from the line for positive λ, per §6.3 p.242 ("pointed away from the
axis for positive charges"). ✔
