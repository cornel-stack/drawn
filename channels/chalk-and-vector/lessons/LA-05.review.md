# Fact-check — LA-05

Source: Jim Hefferon, *Linear Algebra* (CC BY-SA 3.0), §1.II "Linear Geometry of
n-Space" (subsections II.1 "Vectors in Space" and II.2 "Length and Angle
Measures"). Local extract:
`sources/text/hefferon-linear-algebra/002-linear-geometry.md` (PDF pages 45-59
of the book / §1.II pages 35-49; line numbers below are into that extract). The
lesson explains the material in its own words; both worked problems use our own

## Theory scenes s2-s3

**A vector = magnitude + direction, drawn as an arrow (`colEq`, reading 1).**

> "An object in an Rn that is comprised of a magnitude and a direction is a
> vector ... We can draw a vector as having some length and pointing in some
> direction." — §1.II.1, p. 36, lines 49-52

**Point <-> vector, canonical position (`colEq`, reading 2).**

> "We often draw the arrow as starting at the origin, and we then say it is in
> the canonical position ... When v = (v1;v2) is in canonical position then it
> extends from the origin to the endpoint (v1, v2)." — §1.II.1, p. 37, lines
> 83-90

> "We aren't too careful about distinguishing between a point and the vector
> whose canonical representation ends at that point." — §1.II.1, p. 38, lines
> 172-174

The narration's "deliberately blur the line between the vector and that
endpoint" is this identification.

**Column of numbers; Rn as a list of n reals (`colEq`, reading 3).**

> The 'one over and two up' arrow is written as the column (1;2). — §1.II.1,
> p. 37, lines 78-82

> "Rn = {(v1;...;vn) | v1,...,vn in R}" — §1.II.1, p. 38, lines 175-184

The narration's "in four dimensions, or four hundred, a vector is simply a list
of that many numbers" is the obvious generalisation Hefferon states: "We can
extend to R3, or to even higher-dimensional spaces where we have no pictures,
with the obvious generalization" (p. 38, lines 160-162).

**Free vectors — same length and direction are equal regardless of start point
(`threeNote` scene, narration).**

> "those vectors are not just alike, they are equal ... They are equal because
> they have equal lengths and equal directions." — §1.II.1, p. 36, lines 54-57

> "When we want to emphasize this property vectors have of not being anchored we
> refer to them as free vectors. Thus, these free vectors are equal, as each is
> a displacement of one over and two up." — §1.II.1, p. 37, lines 62-64

> "Think of a vector as representing a displacement (the word 'vector' is Latin
> for 'carrier' or 'traveler')." — §1.II.1, p. 36, lines 58-59

The narration's "slide it elsewhere without turning or stretching it ... it is
the same vector" and "what a vector records is a displacement" are exactly this.
(`threeNote` is a text item, not `latex`, so it is not in the scaffolded
equation list; grounded here for completeness.)

**Vector addition component-wise = tip-to-tail / parallelogram (`addEq`).**

> "(v1;v2) + (w1;w2) = (v1+w1; v2+w2)" — §1.II.1, p. 37, lines 118-131

> "where v and w represent displacements, v+w represents those displacements
> combined." — §1.II.1, p. 37, lines 140-144

> "Another way to understand the vector sum is with the parallelogram rule ...
> the sum v+w extends along the diagonal to the far corner." — §1.II.1, p. 38,
> lines 153-158

Our `addEq` form and the narration's "nose-to-tail rule, the diagonal of the
parallelogram" match.

**Scalar multiplication scales each component / stretches the arrow (`scaleEq`).**

> "r.(v1;v2) = (rv1;rv2)" — §1.II.1, p. 37, lines 108-117

> "if v represents a displacement then 3v represents a displacement in the same
> direction but three times as far and -1v represents a displacement of the same
> distance as v but in the opposite direction." — §1.II.1, p. 37, lines 133-139

Our `scaleEq` and "doubling the vector ... stretches the arrow to twice its
length and doubles each number" are this with r=2. The subtraction remark
("reverse w into minus w and add") is the -1 case above, applied component-wise.
All **ok**.

## s4 — worked problem: compute 2v + w (`given`, `step1`, `step2`, `answer`, check)

Our own vectors v=(3;1), w=(-1;2) (`given`); scale v by 2 (`step1`), add w
entry-by-entry (`step2`), boxed answer (5;4) (`answer`). The gate re-runs
`check.expr` `(2*np.array([3,1]) + np.array([-1,2]))[0]`, declared `value: 5.0`.

```python
import numpy as np
v = np.array([3,1]); w = np.array([-1,2])
print("2v =", 2*v)
print("2v + w =", 2*v + w)
print("s4 check expr:", (2*np.array([3,1]) + np.array([-1,2]))[0])
```

output:
```
2v = [6 2]
2v + w = [5 4]
s4 check expr: 5
```

So 2v = (6,2), 2v+w = (5,4) — the boxed answer is exact. `check.expr` returns
`5`, matching `value: 5.0` (tol 1%). **Verdict: ok.**

## s5 — worked problem: length of (2,3,6) (`given2`, `normEq`, `step3`, `answer2`, check)

Our own 3D vector a=(2;3;6) (`given2`); length by Definition 2.1 (`normEq`):
sqrt(2^2+3^2+6^2) = sqrt(4+9+36) = sqrt(49) (`step3`) = 7 (`answer2`). The gate
re-runs `check.expr` `np.linalg.norm(np.array([2,3,6]))`, declared `value: 7.0`.

```python
import numpy as np
a = np.array([2,3,6])
print("2^2+3^2+6^2 =", 2**2+3**2+6**2)
print("sqrt(49) =", np.sqrt(49))
print("s5 check expr:", np.linalg.norm(a))
print("sanity |(3,4)| =", np.linalg.norm(np.array([3,4])))
```

output:
```
2^2+3^2+6^2 = 49
sqrt(49) = 7.0
s5 check expr: 7.0
sanity |(3,4)| = 5.0
```

So |(2,3,6)| = sqrt(49) = 7 exactly, and the 2D sanity case |(3,4)| = 5 confirms
the Pythagorean reading. `check.expr` returns `7.0`, matching `value: 7.0` (tol
1%). **Verdict: ok.**
