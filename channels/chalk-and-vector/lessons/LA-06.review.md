# Fact-check — LA-06

Source: Jim Hefferon, *Linear Algebra* (CC BY-SA 3.0), §1.II "Linear Geometry of
n-Space", subsection II.2 "Length and Angle Measures". Local extract:
`sources/text/hefferon-linear-algebra/002-linear-geometry.md` (book pages 42-49;
line numbers below are into that extract). The lesson explains the material in
its own words; both worked problems use our own numbers. The **work application
(`workEq`, and Problem s5 that uses it) is not in Hefferon — it is our own
cross-disciplinary extension into physics**, grounded in Hefferon's
projection/length-times-cosine reading of the dot product and clearly flagged
below.

## Theory scenes s2-s3

**Dot product as length times cosine (`dotEq`), and the angle formula (`cosEq`).**

> "The angle between two nonzero vectors u,v in Rn is θ = arccos(u•v / |u||v|)."
> — §1.II.2, Definition 2.7, p. 46, lines 888-890

Rearranging gives both forms: cos θ = (a•b)/(|a||b|) (`cosEq`) and a•b =
|a||b| cos θ (`dotEq`). Hefferon derives the same expression from the Law of
Cosines applied to the triangle with sides u, v, u−v:

> "Apply the Law of Cosines: |u−v|² = |u|² + |v|² − 2|u||v| cos θ where θ is the
> angle between the vectors ... Canceling squares ... and dividing by 2 gives a
> formula for the angle. θ = arccos((u1v1 + u2v2 + u3v3)/(|u||v|))" — §1.II.2,
> p. 44, lines 781-804

This is exactly the narration's claim that the cosine form "follows from the law
of cosines" and that the cross terms collect into the component sum. The
Cauchy-Schwarz Corollary guarantees the arccos is defined:

> "2.6 Corollary (Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality) For any u,v in Rn, |u•v| ≤ |u||v|"
> — §1.II.2, p. 45, lines 875-876

> "The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality assures us that the next definition makes sense
> because the fraction has absolute value less than or equal to one." — §1.II.2,
> p. 45-46, lines 883-884

**Component definition (`compEq`).**

> "2.3 Definition The dot product (or inner product or scalar product) of two
> n-component real vectors is the linear combination of their components.
> u•v = u1v1 + u2v2 + ... + unvn" — §1.II.2, Definition 2.3, p. 43-44, lines
> 808-810

Our `compEq` a•b = a1 b1 + a2 b2 is this definition at n=2. The narration's
remark that the two formulas (component and cosine) "always give the same number"
is precisely why Hefferon introduces Def 2.3 right after deriving the Law-of-
Cosines angle formula — the numerator there *is* the component sum (p. 44, lines
805-810).

**Length squared (`v•v = |v|²`) — the narration's "a vector dotted with itself
is its length squared".**

> "Note also that dot product is related to length: u•u = u1u1 + ... + unun =
> |u|²." — §1.II.2, p. 44, lines 817-818

**Sign of the dot product ↔ shape of the angle; orthogonality.**

> "2.8 Corollary Vectors from Rn are orthogonal, that is, perpendicular, if and
> only if their dot product is zero." — §1.II.2, p. 46, lines 892-893

Since |a|,|b| > 0, the sign of a•b matches the sign of cos θ: positive ⇒ acute,
zero ⇒ right angle (orthogonal, Corollary 2.8), negative ⇒ obtuse. This is
exactly the narration's three-way reading (and Hefferon's Exercise 2.32, p. 49,
lines 1107-1108, asks for this acute/right/obtuse condition). **All ok.**

**Work (`workEq`) — OUR OWN cross-disciplinary extension, NOT in Hefferon.**

W = F•d is the standard physics definition of work done by a constant force; it
does not appear in Hefferon's linear-algebra text. It rests entirely on the
dot-product-as-projection reading that Hefferon *does* establish: a•b =
|a||b| cos θ projects one vector onto the other (Def 2.7 / Law of Cosines, p.
44-46, lines 802-804 & 888-890). Work takes exactly that projection — only the
component of the force along the displacement does work. Flagged here as an
application beyond the source; the numerical claim is verified in Problem s5.
**ok (extension clearly labelled).**

## Problem s4 — dot product and angle of a=(3,4), b=(2,1) (`given`, `step1`, `step2`, `step3`, `answer`, check)

Our own vectors a=(3;4), b=(2;1) (`given` — not a Hefferon example). Component
dot product (Def 2.3) gives `step1`; lengths (Def 2.1) give `step2`; angle
formula (Def 2.7) gives `step3` and the boxed `answer`. The gate re-runs
`check.expr` `np.degrees(np.arccos((3*2 + 4*1) / (5*np.sqrt(5))))`, declared
`value: 26.57`, tol 1%.

```python
import numpy as np
ab = 3*2 + 4*1
la = np.sqrt(3**2+4**2); lb = np.sqrt(2**2+1**2)
cos_t = ab/(la*lb)
theta = np.degrees(np.arccos(cos_t))
print("a.b =", ab)
print("|a| =", la, " |b| =", lb, "= sqrt(5) =", np.sqrt(5))
print("cos theta =", cos_t)
print("theta deg =", theta)
print("s4 check expr =", np.degrees(np.arccos((3*2 + 4*1) / (5*np.sqrt(5)))))
```

output:
```
a.b = 10
|a| = 5.0  |b| = 2.23606797749979 = sqrt(5) = 2.23606797749979
cos theta = 0.8944271909999159
theta deg = 26.565051177077994
s4 check expr = 26.565051177077994
```

So a•b = 10, |a| = 5, |b| = √5, cos θ ≈ 0.894, θ ≈ 26.57° (the board rounds to
26.6°). The positive dot product confirms the acute angle. `check.expr` returns
26.565, matching `value: 26.57` within tol 1% (0.019% off). **Verdict: ok.**

## Problem s5 — work W = F·d at 60° (`given2`, `step4`, `answer2`, check)

OUR OWN cross-disciplinary (physics) extension with our own numbers F=10 N,
d=4 m, θ=60° (`given2`). Work is the length-times-cosine form of the dot product
(`step4`), W = Fd cos θ = 10 × 4 × cos 60° = 20 J (`answer2`). The gate re-runs
`check.expr` `10 * 4 * cos(pi/3)`, declared `value: 20`, tol 1%.

```python
from math import cos, pi
W = 10 * 4 * cos(pi/3)
print("cos 60 deg =", cos(pi/3))
print("W =", W)
print("s5 check expr =", 10 * 4 * cos(pi/3))
```

output:
```
cos 60 deg = 0.5000000000000001
W = 20.000000000000004
s5 check expr = 20.000000000000004
```

So cos 60° = 0.5, W = 20 J exactly (to floating-point). `check.expr` returns
20.0, matching `value: 20` within tol 1%. A force perpendicular to the motion
(θ=90°, cos 90°=0) does zero work, as the narration states. **Verdict: ok.**
