# Fact-check — MA-11

Lesson MA-11 derives the LM curve: the locus of interest-rate / output pairs at
which the money market clears. It builds on money demand (demand for money
rising with income/transactions and falling with the interest rate, which is the
opportunity cost of holding money) and a fixed real money supply set by the
central bank, and shows the curve slopes up because higher output raises money
demand, forcing the clearing interest rate up. Two worked problems follow with
M/P = 100, L = 0.5Y − 10r, at Y = 300 (r = 5 %) and Y = 400 (r = 10 %).

Source: OpenStax *Principles of Economics 3e*, CC BY 4.0 — §28.3 *How a Central
Bank Executes Monetary Policy*, §28.4 *Monetary Policy and Economic Outcomes*,
and §27.2 *Measuring Money: Currency, M1, and M2*.

## IMPORTANT distinction — what is OpenStax and what is our construction

This follows the OpenStax-vs-standard split established in MA-05 and MA-10 (the
IS curve handled the same way: standard intermediate-macro construction built on
OpenStax Principles foundations).

**FROM OPENSTAX Principles 3e** (cited with quotes + pages below):

- *The demand for money, and that it depends on income/transactions.* Money is
  held to carry out transactions; its liquidity is why people hold it:

  > "Liquidity refers to how quickly you can use a financial asset to buy a good
  > or service. For example, cash is very liquid. You can use your $10 bill
  > easily to buy a hamburger at lunchtime." (§27.2 Measuring Money, p.650)

  M1 is currency + demand deposits + savings — money held on hand for spending
  (§27.2, p.651). More transactions (more output) therefore means more money
  demanded.

- *The demand for money depends on the interest rate — the opportunity cost of
  holding money.* Money held earns little; the interest forgone is its cost:

  > "with higher interest rates, it is relatively more attractive to put those
  > funds in a financial investment than to make an investment in physical
  > capital." (§28.4, The Effect of Monetary Policy on Aggregate Demand, p.680)

  and the market for liquidity has a price:

  > "It is a market, as we explained in Demand and Supply, with supply, demand,
  > and a price." (§28.3, Monetary Policy and Ample Reserves, p.677)

- *The central bank sets the money supply and influences interest rates.*

  > "These take place when the central bank sells or buys U.S. Treasury bonds in
  > order to influence the quantity of bank reserves and the level of interest
  > rates." (§28.3, Open Market Operations, p.674)

  > "A central bank has the power to create money." (§28.3, p.674)

- *A higher money supply lowers interest rates.*

  > "When a central bank buys bonds, money is flowing from the central bank to
  > individual banks in the economy, increasing the money supply in
  > circulation." and the parallel Clear It Up: a purchase of bonds by the
  > central bank "increases bank reserves and lowers interest rates" (§28.3,
  > Clear It Up, p.674)

  > "An expansionary monetary policy will shift the supply of loanable funds to
  > the right from the original supply curve (S0) to S1, leading to an
  > equilibrium (E1) with a lower 6% interest rate" (§28.4, Fig 28.5, p.679)

  > "In expansionary monetary policy the central bank causes the supply of money
  > and loanable funds to increase, which lowers the interest rate" (§28.4, Fig
  > 28.7, p.680)

**STANDARD INTERMEDIATE-MACRO CONSTRUCTION — NOT in OpenStax Principles 3e.**
Flagged clearly, built by us FROM the OpenStax money market:

- the **LM curve** itself — the upward-sloping r–Y locus and the "LM" label
  (`drawLM`, `markA`, `markB` in the s2 diagram, and `lmLogic`);
- the **money-demand function** L = kY − hr (`demandEq`);
- the **algebraic money-market equilibrium** M/P = L(Y, r) (`moneyEq`,
  `moneyEq2`);
- tracing **Y↑ ⇒ money demand↑ ⇒ r↑** across every level of output into a curve
  (`lmLogic`).

OpenStax Principles has the money market and monetary policy only qualitatively
— the loanable-funds diagram (Fig 28.5), open market operations, the federal
funds market "with supply, demand, and a price" — and never builds the LM curve,
never writes a money-demand function, never writes the equilibrium
algebraically, and never names an LM curve. The economics behind it is OpenStax;
the LM curve that assembles it is our standard-macro layer. Nothing is copied.

## Equation `moneyEq` — `M/P = L(Y, r)` (s2 diagram)

PARTLY FROM OPENSTAX (economic content), STANDARD (algebraic form). The money
market clears when the money people want to hold equals the money the central
bank supplies. OpenStax states both halves qualitatively: the central bank sets
the supply (§28.3, p.674, quoted above) and the demand for money is a market
demand with a price (§28.3, p.677, quoted above). Writing that clearing
condition as the real money supply equal to a money-demand FUNCTION of Y and r,
M/P = L(Y, r), is the standard intermediate-macro money-market condition and is
not in OpenStax Principles. `ok` (economic content FROM OpenStax; algebraic LM
form flagged as our layer).

## Equation `moneyEq2` — `M/P = L(Y, r)` (s3 derivation)

PARTLY FROM OPENSTAX, STANDARD — identical to `moneyEq`, restated to open the
derivation. Same split: the central bank fixing the real money supply and money
demand depending on Y and r are OpenStax (§28.3 p.674; §27.2 p.650; §28.4
p.680); the algebraic equilibrium is our standard-model layer. `ok`.

## Equation `demandEq` — `L = kY − hr`

**STANDARD INTERMEDIATE-MACRO CONSTRUCTION — NOT in OpenStax Principles 3e.**
The linear money-demand function L = kY − hr is our layer. The two directions it
encodes are OpenStax qualitatively:

- rises with income / transactions — money is held to buy goods and services
  (§27.2, p.650, quoted above);
- falls with the interest rate, which is the opportunity cost of holding money —
  higher rates make interest-bearing assets relatively more attractive than
  holding money (§28.4, p.680, quoted above).

Collapsing those into the linear form kY − hr (coefficients k, h) is the
standard money-demand specification, not written in OpenStax. `ok` (correctly
flagged as our construction; directional logic verified below).

## Equation `lmLogic` — `Y↑ ⇒ money demand↑ ⇒ r↑` (and the LM curve: `drawLM`, `markA`, `markB`)

**STANDARD INTERMEDIATE-MACRO CONSTRUCTION — NOT in OpenStax Principles 3e.**
The chain Y↑ ⇒ money demand↑ ⇒ r↑ traced into an upward-sloping r–Y curve — the
LM curve — is our layer. OpenStax has the money market and monetary policy only
qualitatively: the loanable-funds diagram where a smaller quantity of funds
corresponds to a higher rate (Fig 28.5, p.679) and the federal funds market "with
supply, demand, and a price" (§28.3, p.677). It never builds an
interest-rate–output locus and never names an LM curve. The single
comparative-statics fact — with the money supply fixed, more money demanded
raises the clearing rate — is OpenStax-consistent, but assembling it across every
level of output into the upward LM curve (point A at low output / low rate, point
B at high output / high rate in the s2 diagram) is our standard-macro
construction. `ok` (correctly flagged). Directional logic verified below.

## Directional check — `lmLogic`, `demandEq` and the LM slope

```python
# L = kY - hr   (money demand)   ;   M/P = L  (clearing)
# => r = (kY - M/P)/h  : the clearing rate rises with Y when h>0
k, h, MP = 0.5, 10.0, 100.0
def r_clear(Y):
    return (k*Y - MP)/h
for Y in (300.0, 400.0):
    print(f"Y={Y:.0f}  money demand at r=0: kY={k*Y:.0f}  clearing r={r_clear(Y):.2f}")
print("Y up => money demand up:", k*400 > k*300)
print("Y up => clearing r up (LM slopes up):", r_clear(400) > r_clear(300))
```

output:
```
Y=300  money demand at r=0: kY=150  clearing r=5.00
Y=400  money demand at r=0: kY=200  clearing r=10.00
Y up => money demand up: True
Y up => clearing r up (LM slopes up): True
```

Raising output raises money demand (kY from 150 to 200); with the real money
supply fixed at 100, the interest rate must rise (from 5 to 10) to clear the
market — so the r–Y locus slopes up, exactly as `lmLogic` and the s2 diagram
(point B at higher output has a higher rate than point A) claim. `ok`.
