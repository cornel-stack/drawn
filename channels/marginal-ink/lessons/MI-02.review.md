# Fact-check — MI-02

Lesson MI-02 is an elasticity toolkit: the definition of price elasticity of
demand, the sign convention, the three regimes (elastic / inelastic / unit),
the midpoint (arc) method, a point-elasticity form, and the total-revenue test —
with three worked problems. Source: OpenStax *Principles of Economics 3e*, §5.1
(pp. 110-114) and §5.3 (pp. 118-125), CC BY 4.0.

**Notes on scope.**

- **All worked numbers are our own.** OpenStax §5.1 works its midpoint example
  on points A/B (prices $60-$70) and G/H, its supply example on apartment rents
  ($650→$700, 10,000→13,000 units), and §5.3 frames revenue around a 15,000-seat
  arena band. None of those numbers appear in this lesson. Our problems use a
  bus-fare arc example ($12,70)→($8,130), a demand equation `Q = 120 - 4P`, and
  a price rise $10→$11 on that same equation — all invented for the lesson, so
  no OpenStax example is copied verbatim (house rule: "numbers changed").
- **The point-elasticity form `E = -b·P/Q` is our own calculus extension.** It
  is the limiting case of the arc formula as the two points coincide. OpenStax
  §5.1 does **not** give a derivative formula; it explicitly distinguishes the
  **slope** (constant, `rise/run`) from the **elasticity** (varying along the
  curve). Our `dQ/dP = -b` is exactly that slope, and multiplying by `P/Q`
  turns it into the elasticity — consistent with OpenStax but not quoted from
  it. Flagged in equation `ptEq` below.
- **Text items `signNote` and `regimes`** carry no `latex`, so they are not in
  the `equations:` block; both are checked in prose under `elaEq` below, as they
  state the sign convention and the three-regime cut-off directly from §5.1.

## Equation `elaEq` — `E = %ΔQ / %ΔP`

OpenStax defines price elasticity of demand verbatim:

> "The price elasticity of demand is the percentage change in the quantity
> demanded of a good or service divided by the percentage change in the price."
> (§5.1, pp. 110-111)

> "Price elasticity is the ratio between the percentage change in the quantity
> demanded (Qd) or supplied (Qs) and the corresponding percent change in
> price." (§5.1, p. 110)

**`signNote`** ("Report demand elasticity as an absolute value — drop the minus
sign") is OpenStax's convention quoted directly:

> "Because price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, price
> elasticity of demand is always a negative number. Therefore, price elasticity
> of demand is usually reported as its absolute value, without a negative
> sign." (§5.1, p. 110)

> "By convention, we always talk about price elasticities of demand as positive
> numbers. Mathematically, we take the absolute value of the result." (§5.1,
> p. 112)

**`regimes`** (`|E|>1 elastic; |E|=1 unit; |E|<1 inelastic`):

> "An elastic demand or elastic supply is one in which the elasticity is greater
> than one ... Elasticities that are less than one indicate low responsiveness
> to price changes and correspond to inelastic demand or inelastic supply.
> Unitary elasticities indicate proportional responsiveness." (§5.1, p. 110,
> Table 5.1)

`ok` — definition, sign convention, and three regimes all quoted from §5.1.

## Equation `midEq` — arc / midpoint formula (divide each change by the average)

> "To calculate elasticity along a demand or supply curve economists use the
> average percent change in both quantity and price. This is called the Midpoint
> Method for Elasticity." (§5.1, p. 111)

The reversibility claim in the narration ("identical whether the price rose or
fell") is quoted directly:

> "The advantage of the Midpoint Method is that one obtains the same elasticity
> between two price points whether there is a price increase or decrease. This
> is because the formula uses the same base (average quantity and average
> price) for both cases." (§5.1, p. 111)

`ok` — the "divide by the average of the two values" rule and its symmetry are
both stated in §5.1.

## Equation `given` — `P_1=12, Q_1=70 → P_2=8, Q_2=130`

Our own bus-fare numbers for an arc-method problem (OpenStax's §5.1 example

## Equation `step1` — `%ΔQ = (130-70)/100 = +60%`

Application of the midpoint numerator: change in quantity over the **average**
quantity. `(130+70)/2 = 100`, `(130-70)/100 = 0.60`. Matches OpenStax's
"average percent change in ... quantity" (§5.1, p. 111; Figure 5.2 Step 3, p.
113). Recomputed below. `ok`.

## Equation `step2` — `%ΔP = (8-12)/10 = -40%`

Midpoint denominator: change in price over the **average** price.
`(8+12)/2 = 10`, `(8-12)/10 = -0.40`. Matches "average percent change in ...
price" (§5.1, p. 111). Recomputed below. `ok`.

## Equation `answer` — `|E| = |60/-40| = 1.5 ⇒ elastic`

`0.60 / -0.40 = -1.5`, absolute value `1.5`. `1.5 > 1`, so elastic per Table 5.1
("elasticity ... greater than one ... elastic", §5.1 p. 110). The narration's
claim that running it backwards gives the same 1.5 is the reversibility quoted
under `midEq`. Recomputed below, including the reverse direction. `ok`.

## Equation `given2` — `Q = 120 - 4P, P = 20`

Our own linear demand equation for the point-elasticity problem. OpenStax works
point-by-point on figures, not on an algebraic `Q(P)`, so this is our own
rendering with invented coefficients. `ok`.

## Equation `ptEq` — `E = (dQ/dP)·(P/Q) = -b·(P/Q)`

**Our own calculus extension** — the limiting case of the arc formula as the two
points coincide. OpenStax does **not** state a derivative form; it instead warns
against confusing the slope with the elasticity:

> "It is a common mistake to confuse the slope of either the supply or demand
> curve with its elasticity. The slope is the rate of change in units along the
> curve ... The price elasticity, however, changes along the curve." (§5.1,
> "Is the elasticity the slope?", p. 114)

Our `dQ/dP = -b` is precisely that constant slope; multiplying by `P/Q` converts
it into the (varying) elasticity, which is consistent with — but not quoted
from — OpenStax. `ok` as a standard, correctly derived extension, flagged as
non-verbatim.

Recomputed below. `ok`.

## Equation `step1c` — `TR: 10×80 = 800 → 11×76 = 836`

`Q(10) = 120 - 40 = 80`, `TR = 800`; `Q(11) = 120 - 44 = 76`, `TR = 836`. Direct
application of `TR = P × Q` at each price. Recomputed below. `ok`.

## Equation `answer3` — `ΔTR = +36 ⇒ inelastic`

`836 - 800 = +36`. Revenue rose on a price rise, which is the inelastic case of
the revenue test, quoted verbatim from Table 5.3:

> "Inelastic — A given % rise in P will cause a smaller % fall in Q so that
> total revenue (P × Q) rises." (§5.3, Table 5.3, p. 119-120)

For contrast, the elastic case:

> "Elastic — A given % rise in P will be more than offset by a larger % fall in
> Q so that total revenue (P × Q) falls." (§5.3, Table 5.3, p. 119-120)

> "Unitary — A given % rise in P will be exactly offset by an equal % fall in Q
> so that total revenue (P × Q) is unchanged." (§5.3, Table 5.3, p. 119-120)

The narration also classifies this neighbourhood as inelastic; the midpoint
`|E|` between `(10,80)` and `(11,76)` is `≈ 0.54 < 1`, confirming it (computed
below). `ok`.

## Problem `s4` — arc method (bus fare)

```python
# (a) arc elasticity, (12,70) -> (8,130), and the reverse
num = (130-70)/((130+70)/2)   # %dQ
den = (8-12)/((8+12)/2)       # %dP
E = num/den
print("%dQ =", num, " %dP =", den, " E =", E, " |E| =", abs(E))
num_r = (70-130)/((70+130)/2); den_r = (12-8)/((12+8)/2)
print("reverse |E| =", abs(num_r/den_r))
print("check expr =", abs(((130-70)/((130+70)/2)) / ((8-12)/((8+12)/2))))
```

output:

```
reverse |E| = 1.4999999999999998
check expr = 1.4999999999999998
```

`|E| = 1.5` (both directions), matching the boxed `answer`. `1.5 > 1` → elastic.
Reversibility confirmed: same `1.5` going ($12,70)→($8,130) and back. The
`check:` block `abs(((130-70)/((130+70)/2)) / ((8-12)/((8+12)/2)))` returns
`1.5` (within tol 0.01 of value 1.5). `ok`.
