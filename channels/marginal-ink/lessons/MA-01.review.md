# Fact-check — MA-01

Lesson MA-01 teaches gross domestic product: its definition, the expenditure
components `GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)`, the production and income approaches and
why all three agree, the double-counting / final-vs-intermediate distinction,
and a worked expenditure-approach GDP calculation. Source: OpenStax *Principles
of Economics 3e*, §19.1 "Measuring the Size of the Economy: Gross Domestic
Product" (PDF pp. 449-457; source-file lines cited below), CC BY 4.0.

**Note on the income/identity equations.** OpenStax presents the **income
approach and the three-way identity in prose only** — it gives no numbered
income equation. The lesson's `incomeEq` (`GDP = wages + interest + rent +
profit`) and `identEq` (`GDP_spending = GDP_production = GDP_income`) are
**faithful paraphrases of that prose**, so the citations below quote the prose
lines rather than an equation number. All verdicts are `ok` on that
understanding.

## Equation `gdpEq` — `GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)`

OpenStax splits demand into exactly these four parts and then states the GDP
formula built from them:

> "Who buys all of this production? We can divide this demand into four main
> parts: consumer spending (consumption), business spending (investment),
> government spending on goods and services, and spending on net exports."
> (§19.1, p.449; source file line 45-46)

> "Based on these four components of demand, we can measure GDP as:" (§19.1,
> p.452; source file line 172 — the rendered formula image is `GDP = C + I + G +
> (X − M)`)

The four accents in the lesson (C consumption, I investment, G government, NX
net exports) are exactly OpenStax's four components. The definition narrated in
s2 also matches:

> "gross domestic product (GDP), which is the value of all final goods and
> services produced within a country in a given year." (§19.1, p.449; line 34)

The component shares narrated in s2 (consumption ≈ two-thirds, investment ≈ a
sixth, government ≈ a fifth) match the text and Table 19.1:

> "Consumption expenditure by households is the largest component of GDP,
> accounting for about two-thirds of the GDP in any year." (p.451; line 96)

> "Investment demand … typically accounting for only about 15–18% of GDP"
> (p.451; line 102-103) — "roughly a sixth" (~16.7%) is faithful.

> "Government expenditure in the United States is close to 20% of GDP" (p.451;
> line 109-110) — "close to a fifth" is faithful.

Transfer-payment exclusion narrated in s2 also matches:

> "A significant portion of government budgets consists of transfer payments
> … The government excludes these payments from GDP because it does not receive
> a new good or service in return" (p.451; line 114-116).

`ok` — faithful statement of OpenStax's demand-side GDP formula and component
descriptions.

## Equation `incomeEq` — `GDP = wages + interest + rent + profit`

Faithful paraphrase of the **prose** income approach (OpenStax gives no numbered
income equation):

> "Everything a firm produces, when sold, becomes revenues to the firm.
> Businesses use revenues to pay their bills: Wages and salaries for labor,
> interest and dividends for capital, rent for land, profit to the entrepreneur,
> etc. So adding up all the income produced in a year provides a second way of
> measuring GDP." (§19.1 'Another Way to Measure GDP: The National Income
> Approach', p.455; source file line 235-237)

> "The total value of a nation's output is equal to the total value of a
> nation's income." (p.455; line 238)

The four income categories (wages, interest, rent, profit) are exactly the four
listed. `ok` — faithful paraphrase of the prose, cited as prose per the note
above.

## Equation `identEq` — `GDP_spending = GDP_production = GDP_income`

Faithful paraphrase of the **prose** three-way identity (no numbered equation in
the source). OpenStax states each pairing in words:

> "Each of the market transactions that enter into GDP must involve both a buyer
> and a seller. We can measure an economy's GDP either by the total dollar value
> of what consumers purchase in the economy, or by the total dollar value of
> what is the country produces. There is even a third way, as we will explain
> later." (§19.1, p.449; source file line 41-43)

> "Since every market transaction must have both a buyer and a seller, GDP must
> be the same whether measured by what is demanded or by what is produced."
> (p.453; line 206-207)

> "total GDP measured according to what is produced is exactly the same as the
> GDP measured by looking at the five components of demand." (p.453; line
> 178-179)

> "The total value of a nation's output is equal to the total value of a
> nation's income." (p.455; line 238)

Together these three lines give spending = production = income — exactly
`identEq`. The s3 narration's "every single market transaction has two sides at
once … the same ten pounds, seen three ways" is a restatement of the
buyer/seller argument. The production-side facts narrated in s3 also check out:

> "By far the largest part of GDP, however, is services." (p.454; line 219-220)
> and Table 19.2 gives Services $12.7tn = 60.8% of $20.9tn (p.453; line 189-199)
> — the lesson's `pServ` segment value 12.7 and "well over half" are exact.

`ok` — faithful paraphrase of the prose, cited as prose per the note above.

## Equation `finalNote` (s4) — "Count final goods only — never the intermediate parts as well"

The double-counting lorry/truck example and the final-goods rule are taken
directly from the source (with "truck" → "lorry" for British spelling):

> "imagine what would happen if government statisticians first counted the value
> of tires that a tire manufacturer produces, and then counted the value of a
> new truck that an automaker sold that contains those tires. In this example,
> the statisticians would have counted the value of the tires twice—because the
> truck's price includes the value of the tires." (§19.1 'The Problem of Double
> Counting', p.455; source file line 243-246)

> "government statisticians count just the value of final goods and services …
> Statisticians exclude intermediate goods … they will only count the Ford
> truck's value. The value of what businesses provide to other businesses is
> captured in the final products at the end of the production chain." (p.455;
> line 247-252)

The other s4 exclusions narrated (used goods, financial swaps, transfer
payments, home/underground activity) match Table 19.3 and the surrounding prose
(p.456; lines 259-276). `ok`.

chosen so net exports come out **negative**, unlike the source's X=100 < M=120
which also gives −20 but with smaller round numbers). The method is OpenStax's:

> "Step 1. To calculate GDP use the following formula:" (gdpEq) and "Step 2. To
> calculate net exports, subtract imports from exports." (§19.1 'Work It Out',
> p.457; source file lines 328-329)

Recomputation run with the Bash tool (`uv run python`):

