# Fact-check — MA-19

## Source status (FLAGGED — read first)

Intro OpenStax (*Principles of Economics 3e*) does **not** present the formal
**Diamond-Dybvig model** or write the self-fulfilling-run condition, the
two-equilibria structure, or the critical withdrawal fraction as equations. The
lesson header (lines 1–6) states this honestly. This review documents it exactly
as done for MA-18 (Mundell-Fleming): the *mechanism* is covered in intro
OpenStax prose; the *formal construction* (`runEq`, `twoEq`, `f* = R/D`) is the
standard Diamond-Dybvig construction, quoted against the closest supporting
OpenStax prose below.

What IS directly in OpenStax (quoted verbatim per item):

- **The bank-run mechanism** — depositors racing to withdraw; whoever withdraws
  first is paid; even healthy banks can be destroyed by a rumour. (§28.2 Bank
  Runs, p.672; §28.3, p.689.)
- **Fractional reserves, R ≪ D** — a bank holds only a small percentage of
  deposits as reserves and loans the rest out. (§27.4, p.676; §28.2, p.670.)
- **Deposit insurance and lender of last resort are the two protections** — this
  is literally §28.2's learning objective and is developed in §28.3. (§28.2
  p.670; §28.3 p.689–692.)

What is the STANDARD CONSTRUCTION (flagged):

- **`runEq`**  `f·D > R ⇒ failure` — the inequality that a run exceeding reserves
  forces failure. Intro OpenStax says this in words ("a bank run of any size
  would quickly drain any of the bank's available cash … no bank, even those
  that are not bankrupt, can withstand a bank run", §28.3 p.689/p.692); the
  inequality is the standard Diamond-Dybvig construction, flagged in the header.
- **`twoEq`**  confidence → calm, fear → run — two self-consistent equilibria.
  OpenStax states the self-fulfilling dynamic in words ("Even a rumor that a bank
  might experience negative net worth could trigger a bank run and, in a bank
  run, even healthy banks could be destroyed", §28.3 p.689; "Once depositors
  became convinced that the bank would be able to honor their withdrawals, they
  no longer had a reason to make a run", §28.3 p.692). The two-equilibria
  labelling is the standard construction, flagged in the header.
- **`f* = R/D`** (`step1b`/`answer2`) — the critical withdrawal fraction. Grounded
  in the §27.4 fact that reserves are a fraction of deposits (10%, p.676); the
  critical-fraction formula is the standard construction, flagged in the header.

The two **check-blocked numbers** (`s4 answer`, `s5 answer`) are plain arithmetic
on the adapted reserve ratio and are recomputed below.

## Economic reasoning (confirmed against source)

- A run is **self-fulfilling**: the belief that others will withdraw is what
  makes withdrawing rational, so fear alone drains the limited reserves. Source:
  "Even a rumor that a bank might experience negative net worth could trigger a
  bank run and, in a bank run, even healthy banks could be destroyed." (§28.3,
  p.689.) ✔ matches the lesson's claim.
- A **sound bank can fail**: the run, not bad loans, causes the failure. Source:
  "The problem is that bank runs can cause solvent banks to fail." (§28.3,
  p.690); "no bank, even those that are not bankrupt, can withstand a bank run"
  (§28.3, p.692). ✔ matches.
- **Deposit insurance removes the incentive to run**: if deposits are guaranteed,
  there is no reason to race to withdraw, so the panic never forms. Source:
  "Deposit insurance is an insurance system that makes sure depositors in a bank
  do not lose their money, even if the bank goes bankrupt. … Bank runs no longer
  happen at insured banks." (§28.3, p.689–690); "Once depositors became convinced
  that the bank would be able to honor their withdrawals, they no longer had a
  reason to make a run" (§28.3, p.692). ✔ matches.
- No economic claim in the lesson contradicts the source. The only gap is
  **formalization** (writing the mechanism as inequalities/equilibria), which is
  disclosed in the header.
