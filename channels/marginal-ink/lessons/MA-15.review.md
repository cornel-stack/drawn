# Fact-check — MA-15

Lesson MA-15 builds the Taylor rule — the benchmark that describes how a central
bank sets its nominal interest rate as the neutral real rate plus current
inflation plus a response to the inflation gap and the output gap — and
highlights the Taylor principle: the nominal rate must rise more than
one-for-one with inflation so that the real rate climbs and inflation is tamed.
Two worked problems apply the rule with r* = 2, π* = 2, g = 1 at π = 3 (i = 6 %)
and π = 5 (i = 9 %, up 3 points for a 2-point rise in inflation).

Source: OpenStax *Principles of Economics 3e*, CC BY 4.0 — §28.3 *How a Central
Bank Executes Monetary Policy* and §28.4 *Monetary Policy and Economic
Outcomes*.

## IMPORTANT distinction — what is OpenStax and what is our construction

This follows the OpenStax-vs-standard split established in MA-11 (the LM curve
handled the same way: standard construction built on OpenStax Principles
foundations). The split here is cleaner — the *behaviour* is OpenStax, the
*formula* is not.

**FROM OPENSTAX Principles 3e** (cited with quotes + pages below):

- *Central banks raise interest rates to fight inflation and lower them to fight
  unemployment; monetary policy responds to inflation and the state of the
  economy.*

  > "Through the episodes here, the Federal Reserve typically reacted to higher
  > inflation with a contractionary monetary policy and a higher interest rate,
  > and reacted to higher unemployment with an expansionary monetary policy and
  > a lower interest rate." (§28.4, Fig 28.8, p.681)

  > "The Fed should loosen monetary policy when a recession has caused
  > unemployment to increase and tighten it when inflation threatens." (§28.4,
  > p.680)

- *Contractionary policy raises rates when the economy is overheating / inflation
  is high.*

  > "if an economy is producing at a quantity of output above its potential GDP,
  > a contractionary monetary policy can reduce the inflationary pressures for a
  > rising price level ... A contractionary monetary policy will raise interest
  > rates, discourage borrowing for investment and consumption spending" (§28.4,
  > p.680)

  > "A monetary policy that raises interest rates and reduces borrowing in the
  > economy is a contractionary monetary policy or tight monetary policy."
  > (§28.4, p.679)

- *The central bank executes this by moving the federal funds rate through open
  market operations.*

  > "These take place when the central bank sells or buys U.S. Treasury bonds in
  > order to influence the quantity of bank reserves and the level of interest
  > rates." (§28.3, Open Market Operations, p.674)

- *Historical motivation for the Taylor principle — the 1970s inflation and the
  tightening that broke it.*

  > "The rate of inflation was very high, exceeding 10% in 1979 and 1980, so the
  > Federal Reserve used tight monetary policy to raise interest rates, with the
  > federal funds rate rising from 5.5% in 1977 to 16.4% in 1981." (§28.4,
  > Episode 1, p.681)

**STANDARD CONSTRUCTION — NOT in OpenStax Principles 3e.** Flagged clearly,
built by us on the OpenStax policy foundations:

- the **Taylor rule formula** i = r* + π + 0.5(π − π*) + 0.5g — John Taylor's
  rule (`taylorEq`, `taylorEq2`, and the s4/s5 problem items);
- **John Taylor's rule** as a named, quantitative object (the two gap terms with
  their 0.5 coefficients);
- the **Taylor principle** Δi/Δπ = 1.5 > 1 — the nominal rate rising more than
  one-for-one with inflation so the real rate rises (`principleEq`).

OpenStax Principles 3e discusses the *qualitative* behaviour — rates up to fight
inflation, down to fight unemployment, tightening when the economy overheats —
and the loanable-funds/federal-funds mechanism, but it never gives the Taylor
rule equation, never names the rule, and never writes the Taylor principle. The
economics behind the rule is OpenStax; the Taylor rule and the Taylor principle
that formalise it are our standard-macro layer. The numbers in the problems are
our own illustrative figures. Nothing is copied.

## Equation `taylorEq` — `i = r* + π + 0.5(π − π*) + 0.5g` (s2 diagram)

**STANDARD CONSTRUCTION — NOT in OpenStax Principles 3e.** The Taylor rule. The
behaviour it encodes is OpenStax: rates rise with inflation and when the economy
runs hot, fall with unemployment (§28.4 Fig 28.8, p.681, quoted above; §28.4
p.680, quoted above). Writing that as a four-piece formula — neutral real rate,
plus inflation, plus a response to the inflation gap, plus a response to the
output gap — is John Taylor's rule and is not in OpenStax. `ok` (behaviour FROM
OpenStax; formula flagged as the standard formalisation; coefficients ours).

## Equation `principleEq` — `Δi/Δπ = 1.5 > 1` (the Taylor principle, s3)

**STANDARD CONSTRUCTION (the TAYLOR PRINCIPLE) — NOT in OpenStax Principles 3e.**
A one-point rise in inflation raises the nominal rate by 1.5 points (from the 1
on π plus the 0.5 on the inflation gap), so the real rate (nominal − inflation)
rises — the mechanism that genuinely discourages spending and brings inflation
down. OpenStax has the qualitative version only: tight policy raised rates
sharply to break the 1970s inflation (§28.4 Episode 1, p.681, quoted above), and
loose-then-overreaction can feed inflation (§28.4 p.680). The principle itself —
more-than-one-for-one as the stability condition — is the standard formalisation,
not written in OpenStax, and the 1.5 is our Taylor-rule coefficient. `ok`
(correctly flagged; arithmetic verified below).

## Problem `s4` — Taylor-rule rate at π = 3

```python
# Taylor rule: i = r* + pi + 0.5(pi - pi*) + 0.5 g
r_star, pi, pi_star, g = 2, 3, 2, 1
print("s4: 0.5(pi - pi*) =", 0.5*(pi - pi_star))
print("s4: 0.5 g =", 0.5*g)
print("s4: i =", r_star + pi + 0.5*(pi - pi_star) + 0.5*g)
print("s4 check expr 2 + 3 + 0.5*(3 - 2) + 0.5*1 =", 2 + 3 + 0.5*(3 - 2) + 0.5*1)
```

output:
```
s4: 0.5(pi - pi*) = 0.5
s4: 0.5 g = 0.5
s4: i = 6.0
s4 check expr 2 + 3 + 0.5*(3 - 2) + 0.5*1 = 6.0
```

**Verdict `ok`.**
- `given`: r* = 2, π = 3, π* = 2, g = 1.
- `step1`: i = 2 + 3 + 0.5(3 − 2) + 0.5(1) — the rule with numbers substituted.
- `step2`: = 2 + 3 + 0.5 + 0.5 — inflation-gap term 0.5, output-gap term 0.5.
- `answer`: i = 6 % — matches.

## Problem `s5` — Taylor-rule rate at π = 5 (the Taylor principle in action)

```python
r_star, pi_star, g = 2, 2, 1
pi = 5
print("s5: inflation gap pi - pi* =", pi - pi_star, "-> half =", 0.5*(pi - pi_star))
print("s5: i =", r_star + pi + 0.5*(pi - pi_star) + 0.5*g)
print("s5 check expr 2 + 5 + 0.5*(5 - 2) + 0.5*1 =", 2 + 5 + 0.5*(5 - 2) + 0.5*1)
i4 = 2 + 3 + 0.5*(3 - 2) + 0.5*1
i5 = 2 + 5 + 0.5*(5 - 2) + 0.5*1
print("inflation rose by", 5-3, "points; rate rose by", i5-i4, "points")
print("more than one-for-one (Taylor principle):", (i5-i4) > (5-3))
print("di/dpi =", (i5-i4)/(5-3))
```

output:
```
s5: inflation gap pi - pi* = 3 -> half = 1.5
s5: i = 9.0
s5 check expr 2 + 5 + 0.5*(5 - 2) + 0.5*1 = 9.0
inflation rose by 2 points; rate rose by 3.0 points
more than one-for-one (Taylor principle): True
di/dpi = 1.5
```

**Verdict `ok`.**
- `given2`: r* = 2, π = 5, π* = 2, g = 1.
- `step1b`: i = 2 + 5 + 0.5(5 − 2) + 0.5(1) — the rule at π = 5.
- `step2b`: = 2 + 5 + 1.5 + 0.5 — inflation gap now 3, its half 1.5.
- `answer2`: i = 9 % (up 3 for Δπ = 2) — matches. Inflation rose from 3 to 5
  (2 points) and the rate rose from 6 to 9 (3 points): 1.5 points of rate per
  point of inflation, more than one-for-one — the Taylor principle.
- `check.expr` `2 + 5 + 0.5*(5 - 2) + 0.5*1` = 9 (value 9, unit %, tol 1%) —
  matches.
