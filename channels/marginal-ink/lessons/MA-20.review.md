# Fact-check — MA-20

All content is standard intro macro from OpenStax Principles of Economics 3e,
Chapter 24 (The Aggregate Demand/Aggregate Supply Model) and Chapter 25 (The
Keynesian Perspective), plus the multiplier appendix. CC BY 4.0. §24.1 **names
both laws verbatim**: Say's Law = "Supply creates its own demand", Keynes' Law =
"Demand creates its own supply". The conceptual board items are not
formalisation gaps — each is a quoted claim from the source. No economic claim
was found to be wrong.

## Equation `zoneEq`  — flat demand-led vs steep supply-led

> "Consider the SRAS curve's three zones which Figure 24.11 identifies: the
> Keynesian zone, the neoclassical zone, and the intermediate zone." — §24.6,
> p.599

> "Focus first on the Keynesian zone, that portion of the SRAS curve on the far
> left which is relatively flat. ... In the Keynesian zone, AD largely
> determines the [output] ... However, inflationary price pressure is not much
> of a worry in the Keynesian zone, since the price level does [not change
> much]." — §24.6, p.599

> "Now, focus your attention on the neoclassical zone of the SRAS curve, which
> is the near-vertical portion ... the size of potential GDP pretty much
> determines the level of output in the economy." — §24.6, p.599

Also §24.2, p.585: "An AS curve's slope changes from nearly flat at its far
left to nearly vertical at its far right." The lesson's "flat: demand-led |
steep: supply-led" is a direct restatement. **ok.**

## Equation `priceEq`  — the core question: flexible vs sticky prices

The division between the schools is the speed of price/wage adjustment. The
classical/neoclassical side assumes flexible prices so the economy self-corrects
(Say's Law, long run); the Keynesian side assumes sticky prices.

> "Say's law that supply creates its own demand does seem a good approximation
> for the long run." — §24.1, p.582 (flexible-price, self-correcting world)

> "prices and wages are \"sticky,\" making it difficult to restore the economy to
> full employment and potential GDP." — §25.2

The lesson frames the whole disagreement as "prices: flexible? ↔ sticky?", which
is exactly this contrast. **ok.**

## Equation `sayEq`  — Say's Law: supply ⇒ demand

> "Say's law is: \"Supply creates its own demand.\"" — §24.1, p.582

> "a given value of supply must create an equivalent value of demand somewhere
> else in the economy. Because Jean-Baptiste Say, Adam Smith, and other
> economists ... were known as \"classical\" economists, modern economists who
> generally subscribe to the Say's law view ... are called neoclassical
> economists." — §24.1, p.582

**ok.**

## Equation `stickyEq`  — P, W sticky ⇒ output falls

> "sticky wages and prices, which are wages and prices that do not respond to
> [a decline in demand]." — §25.2

> "Instead, prices and wages are \"sticky,\" making it difficult to restore the
> economy to full employment and potential GDP." — §25.2

Fig 25.4 ("Sticky Prices and Falling Demand in the Labor and Goods Market")
shows that when demand falls and prices do not adjust, the quantity (output /
employment) falls instead. The lesson's "P, W sticky ⇒ output falls" matches.
**ok.**

## Problem `s4`

```python
mpc = 0.8
k = 1 / (1 - mpc)          # spending multiplier, simple closed form
print("k =", k)
print("check expr 1/(1-0.8) =", 1/(1-0.8))
print("matches boxed 5 within 1% ->", abs(1/(1-0.8) - 5) <= 0.01*5)
```

output:
```
k = 5.000000000000001
check expr 1/(1-0.8) = 5.000000000000001
matches boxed 5 within 1% -> True
```

Economic reasoning: in the flat Keynesian zone the economy is below potential
with idle capacity, so extra demand raises output via the multiplier (§24.6:
"In the Keynesian zone, AD largely determines the [output]"). A £1bn rise in
government spending lifts output by k × £1bn = £5bn. Correct.

## Problem `s5`

output:
```
gap = 0

matches boxed 0 within tol -> True
```

Economic reasoning: output equals potential, so there is no slack. On the
near-vertical neoclassical/classical stretch extra demand cannot raise output
past capacity and spills into the price level — inflation, not growth (§24.6:
near-vertical neoclassical zone; §24.2, p.585: "higher prices for outputs cannot
encourage additional output, because ... labor and machinery ... are fully
employed"). Correct.
