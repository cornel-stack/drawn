# Fact-check — MA-03

Lesson MA-03 teaches the aggregate production function and diminishing returns to
capital: an aggregate production function turns a country's inputs (physical
capital, human capital, technology, and the workforce/labour) into output (GDP);
the per-worker (per-capita) form where output per worker depends on capital per
worker; labour productivity as output per worker/hour and the engine of long-run
living standards; physical capital raising output; human capital and technology
as the determinants of productivity that shift the whole function up; technology
as the largest, "residual" contributor with human capital mattering at least as
much as physical capital; and capital deepening. It then works two numerical
problems on the illustrative function Y = 10√k. Source: OpenStax *Principles of
Economics 3e*, §20.2 "Labor Productivity and Economic Growth" and §20.3
"Components of Economic Growth" (PDF pp. 481-491), CC BY 4.0.

**Note on our standard-macro restatement.** The concrete functional form
**Y = 10√k**, its derivative **MPK = 5/√k**, and the phrase **"marginal product
of capital"** with the **ΔY/ΔK** slope framing are OUR standard intermediate-macro
restatement layered on the source's concepts. §20.2-20.3 state plainly that the
aggregate production function turns inputs into output, that the per-capita
function expresses output per person as a function of capital/human capital/
technology per person, and that "greater physical capital implies more output"
(p.487) — but the source gives **no** √k functional form, **no** MPK symbol or
derivative, and does **not** use the words "marginal product of capital" or
"diminishing returns to capital". The concave curve, the tangent-as-marginal-
product reading, and the falling-slope "diminishing returns" statement are the
conventional textbook formalisation of the source's "more capital implies more
output, but per-capita growth from capital alone cannot continue indefinitely"
narrative (§20.2 "the only way that GDP per capita can grow continually is if the
productivity of the average worker rises or if there are complementary increases
in capital", p.483). All problem numbers are our own.

## Equation `prodEq` — `Y = f(K, H, A)`

The aggregate production function and its inputs are stated directly:

> "To analyze the sources of economic growth, it is useful to think about a
> production function, which is the technical relationship by which economic
> inputs like labor, machinery, and raw materials are turned into outputs like
> goods and services that consumers use. … In macroeconomics, we call the
> connection from inputs to outputs for the entire economy an aggregate
> production function." (§20.2, p.482; source lines 60-64)

> "In the first production function in Figure 20.2 (a), the output is GDP. The
> inputs in this example are workforce, human capital, physical capital, and
> technology." (§20.2, p.482; source lines 67-69)

So output (GDP) is a function of physical capital K, human capital H and
technology A (with the workforce/labour as the fourth input, folded into the
per-worker form below). `ok`.

## Equation `perWorkerEq` — `Y/L = f(K/L)`

The per-capita/per-worker production function is explicit, including that the
labour input is already absorbed when dividing by population:

> "(b) This aggregate production function has GDP per capita as its output.
> Because we calculate it on a per-person basis, we already figure the labor
> input into the other factors and we do not need to list it separately."
> (§20.2 Figure 20.2 caption, p.482; source lines 74-76)

> "To obtain a per capita production function, divide each input in Figure
> 20.2(a) by the population. This creates a second aggregate production function
> where the output is GDP per capita (that is, GDP divided by population). The
> inputs are the average level of human capital per person, the average level of
> physical capital per person, and the level of technology per person—see Figure
> 20.2(b). … A more important reason for constructing a per capita production
> function is to understand the contribution of human and physical capital."
> (§20.3, p.487; source lines 57-64)

Output per worker as a function of capital per worker (holding human capital and
technology fixed) is exactly this per-capita form. The s2 narration's "labour
productivity — the value each person produces" and its long-run primacy match:

> "Labor productivity is the value that each employed person creates per unit of
> their input." (§20.2, p.481; source lines 32-33)

> "Over the long term, the only way that GDP per capita can grow continually is
> if the productivity of the average worker rises or if there are complementary
> increases in capital." (§20.2, p.483; source lines 80-82)

> "A common measure of U.S. productivity per worker is dollar value per hour the
> worker contributes to the employer's output." (§20.2, p.483; source lines
> 83-84)

`ok`.

## Item `shiftNote` — "Human capital and technology shift the whole function up."

The s4 narration and `shiftNote` are supported almost point-for-point by §20.2
(the two determinants of productivity) and §20.3 (growth accounting).

Human capital and technology as the determinants of productivity:

> "The first determinant of labor productivity is human capital. Human capital is
> the accumulated knowledge (from education and experience), skills, and
> expertise that the average worker in an economy possesses." (§20.2, p.481;
> source lines 37-39)

> "The second factor that determines labor productivity is technological change.
> Technological change is a combination of invention … and innovation". (§20.2,
> p.481; source lines 41-42)

Technology includes new ways of organising work (the assembly-line example the
narration uses):

> "It includes new ways of organizing work, like the invention of the assembly
> line … In short, technology comprises all the advances that make the existing
> machines and other inputs produce more". (§20.3, p.487; source lines 46-49)

Technology the largest / residual contributor:

> "The part of growth that is unexplained by measured inputs, called the
> residual, is then attributed to growth in technology." (§20.3, p.489; source
> lines 116-117)

> "First, technology is typically the most important contributor to U.S.
> economic growth. Growth in human capital and physical capital often explains
> only half or less than half of the economic growth that occurs." (§20.3,
> p.489; source lines 121-123)

Human capital matters at least as much as physical capital, with the post-war
rebuild example:

> "Second, while investment in physical capital is essential to growth in labor
> productivity and GDP per capita, building human capital is at least as
> important. … During the war, a large share of Europe's physical capital … was
> destroyed. … However, the powerful combination of skilled workers and
> technological knowledge … rebuilt Europe's productive capacity to an even
> higher level within less than two decades." (§20.3, p.489; source lines
> 124-131)

Capital deepening:

> "When society increases the level of capital per person, we call the result
> capital deepening. The idea of capital deepening can apply both to additional
> human capital per worker and to additional physical capital per worker."
> (§20.3, p.487-488; source lines 66-68)

The narration's "shift the entire production function up … at every level of
capital per worker the economy now gets more output" is the standard upward-shift
reading of these two determinants, with the moving-along-vs-moving-the-curve
distinction mapping onto capital deepening vs technology/human-capital gains.
`ok`. (This item has no schema `id` in the equations list because it is a text
item; its evidence is recorded here for completeness.)
