# Fact-check — MI-04

Lesson MI-04 teaches consumer surplus, producer surplus and total (social)
surplus as areas on the supply-and-demand diagram, and shows how to measure
each triangle with one half base times height, through three worked problems.
Source: OpenStax *Principles of Economics 3e*, §3.5 "Demand, Supply, and
Efficiency" (PDF pp. 74-77), CC BY 4.0.

**Notes on scope.**

- **All worked numbers are our own.** OpenStax §3.5 develops the entire section
  on a single tablet-computer market: equilibrium price $80, equilibrium
  quantity 28 million, with illustrative points J ($90, 20 million) on demand
  and K ($45, 14 million) on supply. The price-control examples use a back-pain
  drug ($600, 20,000; ceiling $400) and movie theatres ($8, 1,800; floor $12).
  None of those numbers appear in this lesson. Our three problems use invented
  linear equations (`P = 20 - Q` / `P = 2 + 0.5Q`, and `P = 12 - 0.5Q` /
  `P = 2 + 0.5Q`), so no OpenStax example is copied verbatim (house rule:
  "numbers changed").

- **The ½·base·height triangle-area formulas are our arithmetic.** OpenStax
  §3.5 describes consumer and producer surplus only qualitatively as areas —
  "the somewhat triangular area labeled by F" (consumer surplus) and
  "the somewhat triangular area labeled by G" (producer surplus), p.74 and
  Figure 3.23 — and never gives an algebraic area formula. Our
  `CS = ½·Q*·(a - P*)` and `PS = ½·Q*·(P* - c)` are the standard geometric area
  of a right triangle applied to the source's "triangular area" description:
  base = equilibrium quantity Q*, height = the vertical gap from the price to
  the demand intercept a (for CS) or from the supply intercept c up to the
  price (for PS). This is standard geometry layered on the source's triangles,
  consistent with — but not quoted from — §3.5. Flagged in `csEq`, `psEq`,
  `step2`, `step3`, `step5`.

- **Text items** (`statement`, `find`, `statement2`, `find2`, `statement3`,
  `find3`) carry no `latex` and so are not in the `equations:` block; they
  restate the problem tasks and are covered by the surplus definitions below.

## Equation `csEq` — `CS = ½·Q*·(a - P*)`

OpenStax defines consumer surplus and locates it as the area above the price,
below demand:

> "The amount that individuals would have been willing to pay, minus the amount
> that they actually paid, is called consumer surplus." (§3.5, p.74)

> "Consumer surplus is the area labeled F—that is, the area above the market
> price and below the demand curve." (§3.5, p.74)

> "The somewhat triangular area labeled by F shows the area of consumer
> surplus, which shows that the equilibrium price in the market was less than
> what many of the consumers were willing to pay." (§3.5, Figure 3.23, p.74)

The ½·base·height form is our arithmetic on that triangle (base Q*, height
a - P*). `ok`.

## Equation `psEq` — `PS = ½·Q*·(P* - c)`

OpenStax defines producer surplus as the mirror-image area below the price and
above supply:

> "The extra benefit producers receive from selling a good or service, measured
> by the price the producer actually received minus the price the producer
> would have been willing to accept is called producer surplus." (§3.5, p.76)

> "producer surplus is the area labeled G—that is, the area between the market
> price and the segment of the supply curve below the equilibrium." (§3.5,
> p.76)

The ½·base·height form is our arithmetic on that triangle (base Q*, height
P* - c). `ok`.

## Equation `given` — `P = 20 - Q` (demand), `P = 2 + 0.5Q` (supply)

Our own downward-sloping demand and upward-sloping supply lines, crossing at an
equilibrium (§3.5, Figure 3.23, point E). OpenStax's section instead uses the
tablet market ($80, 28 million). Numbers changed per house rule. `ok`.

## Equation `step1` — `20 - Q = 2 + 0.5Q ⇒ Q* = 12, P* = 8`

Setting the two prices equal gives the intersection, which §3.5 identifies as
the equilibrium (Figure 3.23). `18 = 1.5Q ⇒ Q* = 12`, then `P* = 20 - 12 = 8`.
Our arithmetic, recomputed below. `ok`.

## Equation `step2` — `CS = ½(12)(20 - 8)`

The consumer-surplus triangle (area F, "above the market price and below the
demand curve", §3.5 p.74) with base Q* = 12 and height a - P* = 20 - 8 = 12.
½·base·height is our arithmetic on the source's F triangle. `ok`.

## Equation `answer` — `CS = 72`

`½·12·12 = 72`. Our recomputation of area F; confirmed in Problem `s3` below.
`ok`.

## Equation `given2` — `Q* = 12, P* = 8, c = 2`

Reuses the same equilibrium for the producer-surplus problem; OpenStax computes
both surpluses on one diagram ("the area F + G", §3.5 p.76). `c = 2` is the
supply intercept of our own supply line. Our numbers. `ok`.

## Equation `step3` — `PS = ½(12)(8 - 2)`

The producer-surplus triangle (area G, "between the market price and the
segment of the supply curve below the equilibrium", §3.5 p.76) with base
Q* = 12 and height P* - c = 8 - 2 = 6. ½·base·height is our arithmetic on the
source's G triangle. `ok`.

## Equation `answer2` — `PS = 36, TS = 72 + 36 = 108`

`½·12·6 = 36`; total surplus is the sum of the two, quoted from §3.5:

> "The sum of consumer surplus and producer surplus is social surplus, also
> referred to as economic surplus or total surplus. In Figure 3.23 we show
> social surplus as the area F + G." (§3.5, p.76)

`72 + 36 = 108`. Our recomputation; confirmed in Problem `s4` below. `ok`.

## Equation `given3` — `P = 12 - 0.5Q, P = 2 + 0.5Q`

Our own equally-steep demand and supply lines (slopes ±0.5), crossing at an
equilibrium (§3.5, Figure 3.23). Numbers ours. `ok`.

## Equation `step4` — `12 - 0.5Q = 2 + 0.5Q ⇒ Q* = 10, P* = 7`

Intersection of the two lines = equilibrium (§3.5, Figure 3.23). `10 = Q`,
`P* = 12 - 0.5·10 = 7`. Our arithmetic, recomputed below. `ok`.

## Equation `step5` — `CS = ½(10)(5) = 25, PS = ½(10)(5) = 25`

CS triangle (area F, §3.5 p.74): base 10, height a - P* = 12 - 7 = 5. PS
triangle (area G, §3.5 p.76): base 10, height P* - c = 7 - 2 = 5. Both areas
are ½·10·5 = 25. ½·base·height is our arithmetic on the source's F and G
triangles. `ok`.

## Equation `answer3` — `CS = PS = 25, TS = 50`

Total surplus = F + G = 25 + 25 = 50, per §3.5 "social surplus ... the area
F + G" (p.76). The lesson's claim that total surplus is maximised at the
competitive equilibrium is the source's efficiency result:

> "Social surplus is larger at equilibrium quantity and price than it would be
> at any other quantity. This demonstrates the economic efficiency of the
> market equilibrium." (§3.5, p.76)

The forward pointer to deadweight loss (pushing quantity off Q* shrinks the
triangles) is also from §3.5:

> "The loss in social surplus that occurs when the economy produces at an
> inefficient quantity is called deadweight loss. In a very real sense, it is
> like money thrown away that benefits no one." (§3.5, p.76)

Our recomputation; confirmed in Problem `s5` below. `ok`.

## Problem `s3` — consumer surplus

```python
# demand P = 20 - Q, supply P = 2 + 0.5Q
a, md = 20.0, 1.0      # demand P = a - md*Q
c, ms = 2.0, 0.5       # supply P = c + ms*Q
Qs = (a - c) / (md + ms)
Ps = a - md*Qs
CS = 0.5 * Qs * (a - Ps)
print("Q* =", Qs, " P* =", Ps, " CS =", CS)
expr = 0.5 * ((20-2)/(1+0.5)) * (20 - (20 - 1*((20-2)/(1+0.5))))
print("check expr =", expr)
```

output:

```
Q* = 12.0  P* = 8.0  CS = 72.0
check expr = 72.0
```

`Q* = 12`, `P* = 8`, consumer surplus `= ½·12·12 = 72`, matching the boxed
`answer`. The `check:` block returns `72.0` (value 72.0, tol 0.01). `ok`.

## Problem `s4` — producer surplus, same market

```python
a, md = 20.0, 1.0
c, ms = 2.0, 0.5
Qs = (a - c) / (md + ms)   # 12
Ps = a - md*Qs             # 8
CS = 0.5 * Qs * (a - Ps)   # 72
PS = 0.5 * Qs * (Ps - c)
TS = CS + PS
print("PS =", PS, " TS =", TS)
expr = 0.5 * ((20-2)/(1+0.5)) * ((20 - 1*((20-2)/(1+0.5))) - 2)
print("check expr =", expr)
```

output:

```
PS = 36.0  TS = 108.0
check expr = 36.0
```

Producer surplus `= ½·12·6 = 36`, total surplus `= 72 + 36 = 108`, matching the
boxed `answer2`. The `check:` block returns `36.0` (value 36.0, tol 0.01). `ok`.

## Problem `s5` — equally steep curves

```python
# demand P = 12 - 0.5Q, supply P = 2 + 0.5Q

Ps = a - md*Qs
CS = 0.5 * Qs * (a - Ps)
PS = 0.5 * Qs * (Ps - c)

print("Q* =", Qs, " P* =", Ps, " CS =", CS, " PS =", PS, " TS =", TS)
expr = (0.5*((12-2)/(0.5+0.5))*(12 - (12 - 0.5*((12-2)/(0.5+0.5))))
        + 0.5*((12-2)/(0.5+0.5))*((12 - 0.5*((12-2)/(0.5+0.5))) - 2))
print("check expr =", expr)
```

```
Q* = 10.0  P* = 7.0  CS = 25.0  PS = 25.0  TS = 50.0

```

`Q* = 10`, `P* = 7`; CS and PS are each `½·10·5 = 25` (equal, because the two
curves have the same steepness, so the triangles are mirror images); total
surplus `= 50`, matching the boxed `answer3`. The `check:` block returns `50.0`
(value 50.0, tol 0.01). `ok`.
