# Fact-check — MA-14

Lesson MA-14 builds the Phillips curve: the short-run inverse (downward-sloping)
relationship between unemployment and inflation discovered by A.W. Phillips; why
it is only a short-run relationship; how rising inflation expectations shift the
whole curve upward (producing 1970s stagflation); why the long-run Phillips curve
is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment, so there is no permanent
tradeoff; and why anchoring expectations restores a usable short-run tradeoff.
Two worked problems use our own illustrative numbers in the standard
expectations-augmented Phillips equation.

Source: OpenStax *Principles of Economics 3e*, §25.3 "The Phillips Curve"
(p.618-621), with the natural-rate and persistent-expectations material from
§24.5 "How the AD/AS Model Incorporates Growth, Unemployment, and Inflation"
(p.596-598), CC BY 4.0.

## What is DIRECT from OpenStax, and what is STANDARD formalisation

Every **qualitative** claim on the board and in the narration is stated
explicitly in OpenStax §25.3 / §24.5:

- the Phillips curve is the inverse tradeoff between unemployment and inflation,
  found by A.W. Phillips (§25.3, p.619; source lines 40-47);
- it was treated as a policy menu in the 1960s (§25.3, p.620; source lines
  76-78);
- exploiting the tradeoff made **both** inflation and unemployment rise — the
  curve shifted — i.e. stagflation (§25.3, p.620; source lines 79-88);
- the shift is driven by supply shocks and by changes in inflation
  **expectations** (§25.3, p.620; source lines 89-94);
- the downward-sloping curve is valid only in the **short run**; over longer
  periods it shifts (§25.3, p.620; source lines 95-98);
- the **natural rate of unemployment** is the baseline rate year-in, year-out
  (§24.5, p.597; source lines 70-71); the long-run constraint is potential GDP /
  full employment (§24.5, p.597; source lines 70-81);
- sustained expected inflation feeds into prices, wages and interest rates
  (§24.5, p.598; source lines 116-121).

The **one formal element added** is the algebraic equation
`π = π_e − β(u − u_n)`. This is the standard Friedman-Phelps
expectations-augmented Phillips curve — the conventional formalisation of exactly
the qualitative content OpenStax gives (expectations shift the curve; no
permanent tradeoff; the long-run curve is vertical at the natural rate).
**OpenStax discusses all of this qualitatively but does not write this
equation.** It is flagged throughout the equation entries above as the standard
formal expression of the source's words, with the slope β and all numbers our
own. The narration says "Bill Phillips"; OpenStax names him "A.W. Phillips" (a
colloquial rendering of the same economist, A. W. H. "Bill" Phillips) — not a
factual error, just an informal first name.

## Equation `phillipsEq` / `phillipsEq2` — `π = π_e − β(u − u_n)`

STANDARD formalisation of OpenStax §25.3 qualitative content (Friedman-Phelps).

OpenStax states the Phillips curve qualitatively:

> "A Phillips curve illustrates a tradeoff between the unemployment rate and the
> inflation rate. If one is higher, the other must be lower." (§25.3 Figure 25.8
> caption, p.619; source lines 46-47)

> "In the 1950s, A.W. Phillips, an economist at the London School of Economics...
> analyzed 60 years of British data and did find that tradeoff between
> unemployment and inflation, which became known as the Phillips curve." (§25.3,
> p.619; source lines 40-44)

The expectations term:

> "there may be a tradeoff between inflation and unemployment when people expect
> no inflation, but when they realize inflation is occurring, the tradeoff
> disappears. Both factors (supply shocks and changes in inflationary
> expectations) cause the aggregate supply curve, and thus the Phillips curve, to
> shift." (§25.3, p.620; source lines 91-94)

The natural rate `u_n`:

> "We call this baseline level of unemployment that occurs year-in and year-out
> the natural rate of unemployment." (§24.5, p.597; source lines 70-71)

OpenStax does **not** write `π = π_e − β(u − u_n)`; this is the standard
algebraic form of that content. Slope β and all numbers are our own. `ok`.

## Equation `expectEq` — `π_e ↑ ⇒ curve shifts up`

DIRECTLY FROM OpenStax §25.3 / §24.5 (qualitative).

> "When policymakers tried to exploit the tradeoff between inflation and
> unemployment, the result was an increase in both inflation and unemployment.
> What had happened? The Phillips curve shifted." (§25.3, p.620; source lines
> 79-83)

> "The second [factor] is changes in people's expectations about inflation... Both
> factors (supply shocks and changes in inflationary expectations) cause the
> aggregate supply curve, and thus the Phillips curve, to shift." (§25.3, p.620;
> source lines 90-94)

> "if inflation has been occurring for several years, people might begin to
> expect a certain level of inflation. If they do, then these expectations will
> cause prices, wages and interest rates to increase annually by the amount of
> the inflation expected." (§24.5, p.598; source lines 118-121)

Stagflation (high inflation + high unemployment together) is §25.3 p.620 (source
lines 84-88). `ok`.

## Equation `longRunEq` — `long run: vertical at u_n`

STANDARD formalisation of OpenStax §25.3 / §24.5. The long-run Phillips curve is
vertical at the natural rate — the formal statement of "no permanent tradeoff."

> "we should interpret a downward-sloping Phillips curve as valid for short-run
> periods of several years, but over longer periods, when aggregate supply
> shifts, the downward-sloping Phillips curve can shift so that unemployment and
> inflation are both higher (as in the 1970s and early 1980s) or both lower."
> (§25.3, p.620; source lines 95-98)

> "Over this longer period of time, the Phillips curve appears to have shifted
> out. There is no tradeoff any more." (§25.3, p.620; source line 74)

The long-run anchor is potential GDP / full employment at the natural rate of
unemployment (§24.5, p.597; source lines 70-81). The verticality is the standard
formal consequence (expectations fully adjust, so u returns to u_n regardless of
inflation); OpenStax gives it as "no tradeoff any more" rather than as a vertical
line in Phillips space. `ok`.

## Problem `s4` — inflation below the natural rate

Our own illustrative numbers in the standard Phillips equation.

```python
# (a) u below natural rate: pi_e=2, beta=0.5, u=4, u_n=6
pi_e, beta, u, u_n = 2, 0.5, 4, 6
pi = pi_e - beta * (u - u_n)
print("gap (u - u_n) =", u - u_n)
print("pi = 2 - 0.5*(4 - 6) =", pi, "%")
print("s4 check expr value:", 2 - 0.5*(4 - 6), "(boxed value 3)")
```

output:
```
gap (u - u_n) = -2
pi = 2 - 0.5*(4 - 6) = 3.0 %
s4 check expr value: 3.0 (boxed value 3)
```

**Verdict `ok`.**
- `given`: π_e = 2%, β = 0.5, u = 4%, u_n = 6% — our own inputs.
- `step1`: π = 2 − 0.5(4 − 6) — substitution into the standard equation.
- `step2`/`answer`: gap = −2, −0.5·(−2) = +1, so π = 2 + 1 = 3% — exact, matches
  boxed value. Unemployment below the natural rate gives inflation one point
  above expectations, as the expectations-augmented curve requires.
- `check.expr` `2 - 0.5*(4 - 6)` = 3.0 (value 3, tol 1%) — matches.

## Problem `s5` — inflation at the natural rate

Our own illustrative numbers; the u = u_n case illustrating the vertical long-run
result.

```python
# (b) u at natural rate: pi_e=2, beta=0.5, u=u_n=6
pi_e, beta, u, u_n = 2, 0.5, 6, 6
pi = pi_e - beta * (u - u_n)
print("gap (u - u_n) =", u - u_n)
print("pi = 2 - 0.5*(6 - 6) =", pi, "%")
print("pi == pi_e ?", pi == pi_e)
print("s5 check expr value:", 2 - 0.5*(6 - 6), "(boxed value 2)")
```

output:
```
gap (u - u_n) = 0
pi = 2 - 0.5*(6 - 6) = 2.0 %
pi == pi_e ? True
s5 check expr value: 2.0 (boxed value 2)
```

**Verdict `ok`.**
- `given2`: π_e = 2%, u = u_n = 6% — our own inputs.
- `step1b`: π = 2 − 0.5(6 − 6) = 2 − 0 — the gap is zero, so the second term
  vanishes.
- `answer2`: π = π_e = 2% — exact, matches boxed value. At the natural rate
  inflation equals expected inflation, which is the long-run vertical Phillips
  curve ("no tradeoff any more", §25.3 p.620; source line 74).
- `check.expr` `2 - 0.5*(6 - 6)` = 2.0 (value 2, tol 1%) — matches.
