# Fact-check — MI-05

Lesson MI-05 teaches price ceilings, price floors and per-unit taxes on one
supply-and-demand diagram, through three worked problems. Source for the
ceiling and floor material: OpenStax *Principles of Economics 3e*, §3.4 "Price
Ceilings and Price Floors" (PDF pp. 70-73), CC BY 4.0.

**Notes on scope.**

- **Tax incidence (scenes s4, s7) is NOT in §3.4 — it is our extension.**
  §3.4 covers only price ceilings and price floors. It contains no per-unit
  tax, no tax wedge, and no discussion of tax incidence or the
  statutory/economic burden distinction. The tax material in this lesson
  (board items `taxEq` P_b - P_s = t, `incEq` buyer share =
  slope_S/(slope_S + slope_D), and the "the inelastic side bears more / who
  writes the cheque doesn't matter" claim, plus problem `s7` and its
  `given3`/`step5`/`answer3`) is a **standard microeconomics result** layered
  on the same supply-and-demand framework §3.4 uses. It is consistent with —
  but not quoted from — §3.4, and is flagged as our own extension in each
  affected entry above. The wedge identity, the irrelevance of statutory
  incidence, and the elasticity split are textbook staples; they are not drawn
  from this particular section.

- **All worked numbers are our own.** §3.4 develops the ceiling on a
  rental-housing market (equilibrium $500, 15,000 units; shifted demand 19,000;
  Table 3.7) and the floor on an unnumbered European-wheat diagram (Figure
  3.22). Our three problems use invented linear equations
  (`Qd = 120 - 10P` / `Qs = 20 + 10P`; `Qd = 100 - 8P` / `Qs = 10 + 7P`;
  `P = 24 - 2Q` / `P = 4 + Q`), so no OpenStax example is copied verbatim
  (house rule: "numbers changed").

- **Text items** (`statement`, `find`, `statement2`, `find2`, `statement3`,
  `find3`) carry no `latex` and so are not in the `equations:` block; they
  restate the problem tasks.

- **A note on `incEq`'s slope convention.** `incEq` writes buyer share =
  slope_S/(slope_S + slope_D). Which side bears more depends on relative
  elasticity: the more inelastic (steeper in price-quantity space) side bears
  the larger share. In problem `s7` demand is the steeper/less-elastic side and
  buyers indeed bear twice the burden (4 vs 2). The precise slope ratio in
  `incEq` is schematic — the load-bearing claim is the qualitative one (more
  inelastic side pays more), which the numbers confirm. Still an extension, not
  §3.4.

## Equation `ceilEq` — `P_c < P* ⇒ shortage = Q_d - Q_s`

OpenStax defines a price ceiling and names the shortage it causes when it binds
below equilibrium:

> "A price ceiling is a legal maximum price that one pays for some good or
> service." (§3.4, p.71)

> "At that price ($500), the quantity supplied remains at the same 15,000
> rental units, but the quantity demanded is 19,000 rental units. In other
> words, the quantity demanded exceeds the quantity supplied, so there is a
> shortage of rental housing." (§3.4, p.72)

> "when the market price is not allowed to rise to the equilibrium level,
> quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied, and thus a shortage occurs."
> (§3.4, p.72)

The condition that a ceiling only bites below equilibrium ("unless a price
ceiling prevents the price from rising ... If the price is not permitted to
rise", Figure 3.21 caption, p.72) and the rent-control example (p.71-72) are
both in the source. The algebraic form shortage = Q_d - Q_s is our restatement.
`ok`.

## Equation `floorEq` — `P_f > P* ⇒ surplus = Q_s - Q_d`

OpenStax defines a price floor and names the surplus it causes when it binds
above equilibrium:

> "A price floor is the lowest price that one can legally pay for some good or
> service. Perhaps the best-known example of a price floor is the minimum
> wage." (§3.4, p.72)

> "Price floors are sometimes called 'price supports,' because they support a
> price by preventing it from falling below a certain level. Around the world,
> many countries have passed laws to create agricultural price supports."
> (§3.4, p.72)

> "policies to keep prices high for farmers keep the price above what would
> have been the market equilibrium level—the price Pf shown by the dashed
> horizontal line in the diagram. The result is a quantity supplied in excess
> of the quantity demanded (Qd). When quantity supplied exceeds quantity
> demanded, a surplus exists." (§3.4, p.73)

The minimum-wage and farm-price-support examples are both in the source
(p.72-73). The algebraic form surplus = Q_s - Q_d is our restatement. `ok`.

## Equation `taxEq` — `P_b - P_s = t`

**NOT in §3.4 — our extension.** §3.4 has no tax content. The identity that the
buyer price minus the seller price equals the per-unit tax is a standard micro
result on the same supply-and-demand diagram. Confirmed numerically in problem
`s7` (wedge = 6 = t). `ok` as our extension.

## Equation `incEq` — `buyer share = slope_S / (slope_S + slope_D)`

**NOT in §3.4 — our extension.** The irrelevance of statutory incidence and the

## Equation `given` — `Q_d = 120 - 10P, Q_s = 20 + 10P, P_c = 3`

Our own linear ceiling market (§3.4 rent-control example, Figure 3.21 / Table
3.7, p.71-72). OpenStax uses $500/$600 rents and 15,000/19,000 units; our
numbers are invented. Numbers changed per house rule. `ok`.

## Equation `step1` — `120 - 10P = 20 + 10P ⇒ P* = 5, Q* = 70`

Setting quantity demanded equal to quantity supplied gives the equilibrium
("the quantity supplied would equal the quantity demanded at the equilibrium
point", §3.4 p.73). `100 = 20P ⇒ P* = 5`, `Q* = 120 - 50 = 70`. Our
arithmetic, recomputed below. `ok`.

## Equation `step2` — `P = 3: Q_d = 90, Q_s = 50`

Evaluating the two schedules at the capped price, mirroring §3.4's evaluation at
the fixed ceiling price ("At that price ($500), the quantity supplied remains
... but the quantity demanded is ...", p.72). `Q_d = 120 - 30 = 90`,
`Q_s = 20 + 30 = 50`. Our arithmetic. `ok`.

## Equation `answer` — `shortage = 90 - 50 = 40`

The excess of quantity demanded over quantity supplied is the shortage (§3.4,
p.72). Boxed value 40; our recomputation, confirmed in problem `s5` below. `ok`.

## Equation `given2` — `Q_d = 100 - 8P, Q_s = 10 + 7P, P_f = 8`

p.73). The source's wheat diagram is unnumbered; our equations are invented.
Numbers ours. `ok`.

## Equation `step3` — `100 - 8P = 10 + 7P ⇒ P* = 6, Q* = 52`

Equilibrium = intersection of the two schedules ("price P0 and quantity Q0 ...

`Q* = 100 - 48 = 52`. Our arithmetic, recomputed below. `ok`.

## Equation `step4` — `P = 8: Q_d = 36, Q_s = 66`

Evaluating the two schedules at the floor price. `Q_d = 100 - 64 = 36`,
`Q_s = 10 + 56 = 66`. Our arithmetic. `ok`.

## Equation `answer2` — `surplus = 66 - 36 = 30`

The excess of quantity supplied over quantity demanded is the surplus ("When
quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded, a surplus exists", §3.4 p.73).
Boxed value 30; our recomputation, confirmed in problem `s6` below. `ok`.

## Equation `given3` — `P = 24 - 2Q (demand), P = 4 + Q (supply), t = 6`

**NOT in §3.4 — our extension.** Our own inverse demand and supply lines with a
per-unit tax of 6 on sellers. No tax content in §3.4. Numbers ours. `ok` as our

## Equation `step5` — `P_b = 14⅔, P_s = 8⅔, P_b - P_s = 6`

**NOT in §3.4 — our extension.** After-tax prices from shifting supply up by the
tax: taxed quantity Q = (24 - 10)/3 = 14/3, so `P_b = 24 - 2·14/3 = 44/3 = 14⅔`,
`P_s = 4 + 14/3 = 26/3 = 8⅔`, wedge = 6 = t. Standard micro, not §3.4. Our
arithmetic, confirmed below. `ok` as our extension.

## Equation `answer3` — `buyer burden = 4, seller burden = 2`

**NOT in §3.4 — our extension.** Against the original price P0 = 32/3 = 10⅔,
buyers pay `14⅔ - 10⅔ = 4` more, sellers keep `10⅔ - 8⅔ = 2` less. The steeper,
less-elastic demand side (buyers) bears twice the burden even though the tax is
levied on sellers. Standard micro incidence result, not §3.4. Our
recomputation, confirmed in problem `s7` below. `ok` as our extension.

## Problem `s5` — ceiling, shortage

```python
# Qd = 120 - 10P, Qs = 20 + 10P, ceiling Pc = 3
P_star = (120 - 20) / (10 + 10)
Q_star = 120 - 10*P_star
print("P* =", P_star, " Q* =", Q_star)
Pc = 3
Qd = 120 - 10*Pc
Qs = 20 + 10*Pc
print("ceiling 3: Qd =", Qd, " Qs =", Qs, " shortage =", Qd - Qs)
print("check expr =", (120 - 10*3) - (20 + 10*3))
```

output:

```
P* = 5.0  Q* = 70.0
ceiling 3: Qd = 90  Qs = 50  shortage = 40
check expr = 40
```

`P* = 5`, `Q* = 70`; at the ceiling of 3, `Q_d = 90`, `Q_s = 50`, shortage
`= 40`, matching the boxed `answer`. The `check:` block
`(120 - 10*3) - (20 + 10*3)` returns `40` (value 40, tol 1%). `ok`.

## Problem `s7` — tax incidence (our extension, not §3.4)

```python
# demand P = 24 - 2Q, supply P = 4 + Q, tax t = 6 on sellers
Q0 = (24 - 4) / (2 + 1)       # original equilibrium quantity
P0 = 24 - 2*Q0
print("original Q0 =", round(Q0,4), " P0 =", round(P0,4))
Qt = (24 - (4 + 6)) / (2 + 1) # taxed quantity: 24-2Q = 4+Q+6
Pb = 24 - 2*Qt
Ps = 4 + Qt
print("taxed Q =", round(Qt,4), " Pb =", round(Pb,4),
      " Ps =", round(Ps,4), " wedge =", round(Pb-Ps,4))
print("buyer burden = Pb - P0 =", round(Pb - P0,4))
print("seller burden = P0 - Ps =", round(P0 - Ps,4))
print("check expr =", (24 - 2*((24-(4+6))/(2+1))) - (24 - 2*((24-4)/(2+1))))
```

output:

```
original Q0 = 6.6667  P0 = 10.6667
taxed Q = 4.6667  Pb = 14.6667  Ps = 8.6667  wedge = 6.0
buyer burden = Pb - P0 = 4.0
seller burden = P0 - Ps = 2.0
check expr = 4.0
```

Original price `P0 = 10⅔`; after a tax of 6 on sellers the buyer price rises to
`14⅔` and the seller price falls to `8⅔`, a wedge of exactly 6. Buyers bear
`4`, sellers bear `2` — twice as much on the inelastic buyers, even though the
tax is levied on sellers. The boxed `answer3` (buyer burden 4, seller burden 2)
matches. The `check:` block (buyer burden = P_b - P0) returns `4.0` (value 4.0,
tol 1%). Confirmed as our own extension, not sourced from §3.4. `ok`.
