# Fact-check — MA-02

Lesson MA-02 teaches inflation and the two headline price-level measures: the
definition of inflation (a general, ongoing rise in the overall price level, not
a relative-price change), the construction of a price index (base year = 100,
index = 100 × cost this year / cost base year), the inflation rate as the
percentage change in the index (not the difference of index numbers), the
Consumer Price Index (a fixed consumer basket including imports), the GDP
deflator (100 × nominal GDP / real GDP, all domestic output with changing
weights), and why the two disagree (different baskets, fixed vs shifting weights,
substitution bias, quality/new-goods bias — so the CPI overstates). It then works
three problems: a basket price index + inflation rate, a GDP deflator, and a
nominal-to-real salary deflation. Source: OpenStax *Principles of Economics 3e*,
§22.1 "Tracking Inflation" and §22.2 "How to Measure Changes in the Cost of
Living" (PDF pp. 530-538), CC BY 4.0.

**Note on freshly chosen numbers.** OpenStax's §22.1 Work It Out uses a basket of
20 hamburgers, 1 bottle of aspirin, 5 movies with Pd-1 total $100 rising to Pd-2
$106.50 (6.5%). All three worked problems in this lesson use **our own figures**
— a basket of 8 coffees, 4 notebooks, 2 textbooks giving £100 → £108 (8%); a GDP
deflator from nominal £26,400bn / real £24,000bn; and a £54,000 salary deflated
at an index of 120 — none copied from the source (see the distinctness
recomputation below). The schema id for the s7 boxed answer is `answer` (shared
with s5's id in the YAML); it is listed here as `answer3` to keep the two
distinct in this review.

## Equation `indexEq` — `index = 100 × (cost this year / cost base year)`

> "To convert the money spent on the basket to an index number, economists
> arbitrarily choose one year to be the base year … The base year, by definition,
> has an index number equal to 100 … Since the total amount of spending in that
> year is $107, we divide that amount by itself ($107) and multiply by 100 …
> Then, to figure out the values of the index number for the other years, we
> divide the dollar amounts for the other years by 1.07 as well. Note also that
> the dollar signs cancel out so that index numbers have no units." (§22.1 'Index
> Numbers', p.533; source file lines 193-200)

Dividing this-year cost by base-year cost and multiplying by 100 is exactly
`indexEq`, and the base year equals 100 by construction. `ok`.

## Item `inflDef` — "Inflation = the percentage change in the price index."

The definition and the relative-price exclusion are stated almost verbatim:

> "Inflation is a general and ongoing rise in the level of prices in an entire
> economy. Inflation does not refer to a change in relative prices. A relative
> price change occurs when you see that the price of tuition has risen, but the
> price of laptops has fallen." (§22.1, p.530; source file lines 17-19)

> "The inflation rate is simply the percentage change in the price level."
> (§22.1, p.531; source file lines 94-95)

`ok` — inflation is the percentage change in the index, and is explicitly *not* a
relative-price change, matching the narration in s2.

## Equation `inflEq` — `inflation = 100 × (index₂ − index₁) / index₁`

The percentage-change formula and the "do not subtract" warning are quoted
directly, including the 107 → 110 = 2.8% example the narration reproduces:

> "the inflation rate is not derived by subtracting the index numbers, but rather
> through the percentage-change calculation. We calculate the precise inflation
> rate as the price index moves from 107 to 110 as 100 x (110 – 107) / 107 = 100
> x 0.028 = 2.8%. When the base year is fairly close to 100, a quick subtraction
> is not a terrible shortcut … but when precision matters down to tenths of a
> percent, subtracting will not give the right answer." (§22.2 'Why do you not
> just subtract index numbers?', p.534; source file lines 13-18)

The s3 narration's "going from 107 to 110 is not 3% … it is 3/107 ≈ 2.8% … divide
by where you started, never just subtract" is this passage restated. `ok`.

## Equation `cpiEq` — `CPI = 100 × (fixed consumer basket this year / its base-year cost)`

The CPI is the §22.1 index formula applied to a **fixed** consumer basket, and
that basket includes imports:

> "Government statisticians at the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics calculate the
> CPI based on the prices in a fixed basket of goods and services that represents
> the purchases of the average family of four." (§22.2, p.534; source file lines
> 48-49)

The imports point (narrated in s4, `cpiImp` "+ imports") is supported by the
contrast OpenStax draws with the deflator, which *excludes* imports: the GDP
deflator "includes all the GDP components (that is, consumption plus investment
plus government plus exports minus imports)" (§22.2, p.538; lines 213-215) —
i.e. net exports, so imports are *subtracted* from the deflator's basket, whereas
the CPI's consumer basket counts the imported goods households actually buy (the
International Price Index "based on the prices of merchandise that is exported or
imported", p.538, line 212, and the Table 22.1 CPI goods such as new cars and
gasoline are routinely imported). `ok`.

## Equation `defEq` — `GDP deflator = 100 × (nominal GDP / real GDP)`

> "The GDP deflator, which the Bureau of Economic Analysis measures, is a price
> index that includes all the GDP components (that is, consumption plus
> investment plus government plus exports minus imports). Unlike the CPI, its
> baskets are not fixed but re-calculate what that year's GDP would have been
> worth using the base-year's prices." (§22.2 'Additional Price Indices', p.538;
> source file lines 213-216)

> "If one is concerned with the most accurate measure of inflation, one should
> use the GDP deflator as it picks up the prices of goods and services produced."
> (§22.2, p.538; source file lines 219-221)

The deflator re-prices each year's actual output against base-year prices — i.e.
nominal GDP (current prices) over real GDP (base-year prices), ×100 — covers all
domestic output with changing weights, and is the broader/more accurate gauge.
This matches `defEq` and the s4 narration. `ok`.

## Item `biasNote` — "A fixed basket misses substitution and new goods — so CPI overstates."

Both biases and the overstatement direction are stated verbatim:

> "substitution bias—the rise in the price of a fixed basket of goods over
> time—tends to overstate the rise in a consumer's true cost of living, because
> it does not take into account that the person can substitute away from goods
> whose relative prices have risen." (§22.2, p.535; source file lines 68-70)

> "the quality/new goods bias means that the rise in the price of a fixed basket
> of goods over time tends to overstate the rise in a consumer's true cost of
> living, because it does not account for how improvements in the quality of
> existing goods or the invention of new goods improves the standard of living."
> (§22.2, p.536; source file lines 99-102)

The s4 narration's "frozen basket … cannot let shoppers substitute away … slow to
credit new or improved products … CPI tends to read a little high" is exactly
these two biases. The magnitude narrated elsewhere is also supported: "the rise
in the CPI probably overstates the true rise in inflation by only about 0.5% per
year" (§22.2, p.538; line 185-186). `ok`.

## Problem `s5` — given / step1 / step2 / answer (basket index + inflation)

Method is OpenStax's fixed-basket "Amount Spent" calculation (§22.1 Table 22.2)
plus the index/inflation formulae; **numbers are our own** (8 coffee, 4 notebook,
2 textbook; base £100 → this year £108). Recomputation:

```python
qty  = {"coffee": 8, "notebook": 4, "textbook": 2}
base = {"coffee": 3.00, "notebook": 4.00, "textbook": 30.00}
now  = {"coffee": 3.30, "notebook": 4.00, "textbook": 32.80}
cost_base = sum(qty[g]*base[g] for g in qty)   # 100.0
cost_now  = sum(qty[g]*now[g]  for g in qty)   # 108.0
index = 100 * cost_now / cost_base
infl  = 100 * (cost_now - cost_base) / cost_base
print("base-year cost =", cost_base)
print("this-year cost =", cost_now)
print("index =", index)          # check.expr, board 108
print("inflation =", infl, "%")  # board 8%
print("textbook share of base cost =", 100*qty['textbook']*base['textbook']/cost_base, "%")
```

output:
```
base-year cost = 100.0
this-year cost = 108.0
index = 108.0
inflation = 8.0 %
textbook share of base cost = 60.0 %
```

**Verdict `ok`.**
- `step1`: base = £100, this year = £108 — matches the board.
- `step2`: index = 100 × 108/100 = 108 — exact.
- `answer`: boxed inflation = 100 × 8/100 = 8% — exact.
- The narration's "textbook, at £60 of the £100, drives most of the rise" is
  confirmed: the textbook is 60% of base-year cost.
