# Fact-check — MI-01

Lesson MI-01 teaches the demand/supply model, the demand vs quantity-demanded
distinction (movement along vs shift of a curve), and a worked gasoline
equilibrium. Source: OpenStax *Principles of Economics 3e*, §3.1 (pp. 64-69)
and §3.2 (pp. 70-79), CC BY 4.0.

**Note on the algebra.** OpenStax does **not** give algebraic demand/supply
functions. It presents the *law of demand* and *law of supply* in words, plus
numeric **schedules** (Table 3.1, Table 3.2, Table 3.3) and downward/upward
sloping **curves** (Figures 3.2-3.4). The lesson's `Q_d = a - bP` and
`Q_s = c + dP` are **our own linear modelling** of those laws and schedules —
a standard pedagogical rendering, not a formula quoted from the text. The
worked numbers `Q_d = 1300 - 500P`, `Q_s = 250 + 250P` are a **linear fit** we
chose so the lines pass through OpenStax's equilibrium ($1.40, 600) and
reproduce the schedule points in the equilibrium neighbourhood (see Problem
`s6` below). All verdicts are `ok` on that understanding.

## Equation `demandEq` — `Q_d = a - b\,P`

Our linear rendering of the **law of demand** (downward-sloping). OpenStax
states the law, not a formula:

> "Economists call this inverse relationship between price and quantity
> demanded the law of demand." (§3.1, p.65)

> "Demand curves embody the law of demand: As the price increases, the
> quantity demanded decreases, and conversely, as the price decreases, the
> quantity demanded increases." (§3.1, p.66)

The downward slope and the demand schedule (Table 3.1: $1.00→800, …,
$2.20→420) confirm the negative price coefficient `-b`. The narration's
"quantity on the horizontal axis and price on the vertical" matches:

> "with quantity on the horizontal axis and the price per gallon on the
> vertical axis." (§3.1, p.65)

`ok` — faithful linear model of a stated law + schedule.

## Equation `supplyEq` — `Q_s = c + d\,P`

Our linear rendering of the **law of supply** (upward-sloping). OpenStax:

> "Economists call this positive relationship between price and quantity
> supplied—that a higher price leads to a higher quantity supplied and a lower
> price leads to a lower quantity supplied—the law of supply." (§3.1, p.66)

> "Nearly all supply curves, however, share a basic similarity: they slope up
> from left to right and illustrate the law of supply." (§3.1, p.67)

Supply schedule (Table 3.2: $1.00→500, …, $2.20→720) confirms the positive
coefficient `+d`. `ok`.

## Equation `eqCond` — `Q_d = Q_s`

> "The equilibrium price is the only price where the plans of consumers and the
> plans of producers agree—that is, where the amount of the product consumers
> want to buy (quantity demanded) is equal to the amount producers want to sell
> (quantity supplied)." (§3.1, p.68)

> "At any other price, the quantity demanded does not equal the quantity
> supplied, so the market is not in equilibrium at that price." (§3.1, p.68)

`ok` — this is OpenStax's equilibrium condition stated verbatim in words.

## Equation `given` — `Q_d = 1300 - 500\,P, \; Q_s = 250 + 250\,P`

Linear fit to OpenStax's gasoline schedules (Table 3.1 / Table 3.2 / Table 3.3,
§3.1 pp. 65-68). Not quoted from the text — see Problem `s6` for the point-by-
point reproduction. The fit is exact at the equilibrium neighbourhood
($1.00, $1.20, $1.40) and preserves OpenStax's stated equilibrium. `ok`.

## Equation `step1` — `1300 - 500\,P = 250 + 250\,P`

Direct application of `eqCond` (`Q_d = Q_s`) to the given linear curves.
Justified by:

> "you could find the equilibrium by looking for the price level on the tables
> where the quantity demanded and the quantity supplied are equal." (§3.1, p.68)

`ok`.

## Equation `step2` — `1050 = 750\,P`

Algebra on `step1`: add `500P` to both sides and subtract `250` →
`1300 - 250 = (500 + 250)P` → `1050 = 750P`. Pure arithmetic rearrangement of
`step1`; recomputed below. `ok`.

## Equation `step3` — `P^* = 1050/750 = 1.40`

> "In Figure 3.4, the equilibrium price is $1.40 per gallon of gasoline."
> (§3.1, p.68)

`1050/750 = 1.40` exactly (recomputed below). Matches OpenStax's stated
equilibrium price. `ok`.

## Equation `answer` — `P^* = \$1.40, \; Q^* = 600`

> "the equilibrium price is $1.40 per gallon of gasoline and the equilibrium
> quantity is 600 million gallons." (§3.1, p.68)

> "The demand curve (D) and the supply curve (S) intersect at the equilibrium
> point E, with a price of $1.40 and a quantity of 600." (Figure 3.4, §3.1 p.68)

Both P* and Q* match OpenStax exactly. Table 3.3 confirms Q_d = Q_s = 600 at
$1.40. `ok`.

## Problem `s6` — equilibrium recomputation

Recomputation run with the Bash tool (`uv run python`):

```python
def Qd(P): return 1300 - 500*P
def Qs(P): return 250 + 250*P

# (a) Equilibrium by algebra: 1300 - 500 P = 250 + 250 P
Pstar = (1300 - 250) / (500 + 250)
Qstar = Qd(Pstar)
print("P* = (1300-250)/(500+250) =", Pstar)
print("Q* = Qd(P*) =", Qstar, "= Qs(P*) =", Qs(Pstar))

# (b) Reproduce OpenStax schedule points (Table 3.1 demand / Table 3.2 supply)
demand_tbl = {1.00:800, 1.20:700, 1.40:600, 1.60:550, 1.80:500, 2.00:460, 2.20:420}
supply_tbl = {1.00:500, 1.20:550, 1.40:600, 1.60:640, 1.80:680, 2.00:700, 2.20:720}
for P in sorted(demand_tbl):
    print(f"{P:.2f}   {Qd(P):7.0f}   {demand_tbl[P]:7d}      {Qs(P):7.0f}   {supply_tbl[P]:7d}")
exactD = [P for P in demand_tbl if Qd(P)==demand_tbl[P]]
exactS = [P for P in supply_tbl if Qs(P)==supply_tbl[P]]
print("Demand points matched exactly:", exactD)
print("Supply points matched exactly:", exactS)
```

**Verdict `ok`.**
- (a) `P* = (1300-250)/(500+250) = 1.40` and `Q* = 600` — matches the lesson's
  boxed answer and OpenStax's stated equilibrium ($1.40 / 600 million gallons,
  Figure 3.4, §3.1 p.68) exactly. `Qd(P*) = Qs(P*) = 600` confirms `eqCond`.
- (b) The linear curves reproduce OpenStax's gasoline schedule points
  **exactly** across the equilibrium neighbourhood $1.00-$1.40 (demand and
  supply both: 800/700/600 and 500/550/600). Above $1.40 OpenStax's real
  schedules bend (its curves are not perfectly linear), so the straight-line
  fit diverges modestly at $1.60-$2.20 — expected and harmless, since the
  lesson uses the fit only to land the **equilibrium** the text states, which
  it does exactly. This is honest linear modelling of the schedule, not a
  misquote.
