# Fact-check — MI-06

Lesson MI-06 builds the indifference-curve / budget-constraint picture and the
utility-maximising tangency rule, then works two Cobb-Douglas examples. Primary
source: OpenStax *Principles of Economics 3e*, **Appendix B "Indifference
Curves"** — the sub-sections *What Is an Indifference Curve?* (PDF pp. 875-876,
printed pp. 859-860), *Utility-Maximizing with Indifference Curves* (p. 877,
printed p. 861), and *Changes in Income* (p. 878, printed p. 862) — together
with **§2.1 "How Individuals Make Choices Based on Their Budget Constraint"**
(pp. 44-49, printed pp. 28-33) for the budget line and its price-ratio slope.
Both CC BY 4.0.

**Notes on scope.**

- **The qualitative framework is from OpenStax.** The appendix supplies: an
  indifference curve = combinations giving equal utility; downward-sloping and
  convex (bowed to the origin) curves because the marginal rate of substitution
  diminishes; higher curves = higher utility; and the optimum at the tangency of
  the budget line with the highest attainable curve. §2.1 supplies the budget
  equation (total spending = income) and the slope = price ratio. All of these
  are quoted below.

- **The Cobb-Douglas specifics are NOT in the appendix — standard micro / our
  parameterisation.** The appendix never writes a utility function. The form
  `U = x^a y^b`, the result `MRS = y/x` for `U = xy`, and the constant
  expenditure-share rule `p_x·x* = a/(a+b)·m` (board item `shareEq`, and the
  `MRS = y/x` used in `step1`) are a **standard intermediate-microeconomics
  result for Cobb-Douglas utility**, layered on top of the appendix's
  qualitative tangency logic. They are consistent with — but not drawn from —
  OpenStax, and are flagged as our own parameterisation in each affected entry.

- **All worked numbers are our own.** The appendix uses Lilly's books/doughnuts
  ($6 / $0.50, income $60) and Manuel/Natasha's yogurts/movies ($1 / $4, income
  $40). Our two problems use invented figures (`U = xy`, `p_x = 4`, `p_y = 2`,
  `m = 24`; `U = x^3 y`, `p_x = 5`, `m = 40`), so no OpenStax example is copied
  verbatim (house rule: "numbers changed").

- **Text items** (`statement`, `find`, `statement2`, `find2`) carry no `latex`
  and so are not in the `equations:` block; they restate the problem tasks.

## Equation `budgetEq` — `p_x·x + p_y·y = m`

§2.1 gives the budget constraint in words and symbols:

> "The equation for any budget constraint is: ... where P and Q are the price
> and quantity of items purchased (which we assume here to be two items) and
> Budget is the amount of income one has to spend." (§2.1, p.30)

> "Each point on the budget constraint represents a combination of burgers and
> bus tickets whose total cost adds up to Alphonso's budget of $10." (Figure 2.2
> caption, §2.1, p.28)

Our `p_x·x + p_y·y = m` is the symbolic restatement (spending on x plus spending
on y equals income). `ok`.

## Equation `mrsEq` — `MRS = MU_x / MU_y`

The appendix defines the MRS as the indifference-curve slope:

> "Indeed, the slope along an indifference curve as the marginal rate of
> substitution, which is the rate at which a person is willing to trade one good
> for another so that utility will remain the same." (Appendix B, *The Shape of
> an Indifference Curve*, p.859)

The source states the MRS verbally (and ties its change to diminishing marginal
utility) but does not write the `MU_x/MU_y` ratio; that algebraic form is the
standard restatement of "the rate one is willing to trade, holding utility
constant". Qualitative definition sourced; ratio notation standard. `ok`.

## Equation `priceEq` — `slope of budget = p_x / p_y`

§2.1 defines the budget-line slope as the price ratio:

> "we define the slope as the price of bus tickets (whatever is on the
> horizontal axis in the graph) divided by the price of burgers (whatever is on
> the vertical axis) ... If you want to determine the opportunity cost quickly,
> just divide the two prices." (§2.1, p.31)

> "The relative price of burgers and bus tickets determines the slope of the
> budget constraint." (Figure 2.2 caption, §2.1, p.28)

With x on the horizontal axis and y on the vertical, slope = p_x/p_y. `ok`.

## Equation `tangEq` — `MU_x / MU_y = p_x / p_y`

The appendix locates the optimum at the tangency of the budget line with the
highest attainable indifference curve:

> "Lilly's optimal choice will be point B, where the budget line is tangent to
> the indifference curve Um. Lilly would have more utility at a point like F on
> the higher indifference curve Uh, but the budget line does not touch the
> higher indifference curve Uh at any point, so she cannot afford this choice."
> (Appendix B, *Changes in Income* opening, p.878)

> "The highest achievable indifference curve touches the opportunity set at a
> single point of tangency." (Appendix B, p.878)

"Tangent" means the two slopes are equal: the indifference-curve slope (MRS =
MU_x/MU_y) equals the budget-line slope (p_x/p_y). The tangency logic is from the
source; the equal-slopes equation is its standard restatement. `ok`.

## Equation `shareEq` — `p_x·x* = a/(a+b)·m`

**NOT in the OpenStax appendix — standard Cobb-Douglas result (our
parameterisation).** The appendix never writes a utility function and never
derives a spending share. The constant expenditure-share rule for Cobb-Douglas
utility `U = x^a y^b` — the consumer spends fraction `a/(a+b)` of income on x —
is a standard intermediate-micro result. It follows from the sourced tangency
condition `MU_x/MU_y = p_x/p_y`, but the functional form and the share formula
are ours, not OpenStax. `ok` as our parameterisation.

## Problem `s4` — utility maximisation, U = xy

```python
# U = xy (so MRS = y/x), px=4, py=2, m=24
px, py, m = 4, 2, 24
ratio = px/py                     # tangency: y/x = px/py
x_star = m / (px + ratio*py)      # 4x + 2(2x) = 8x = 24
y_star = ratio * x_star
print("price ratio px/py =", ratio, " => y =", ratio, "x")
print("x* =", x_star, " y* =", y_star)
print("spend on x =", px*x_star, " spend on y =", py*y_star,
      " total =", px*x_star + py*y_star)
print("s4 check expr (1/2)*(24/4) =", (1/2) * (24 / 4))
```

output:

```
price ratio px/py = 2.0  => y = 2.0 x
x* = 3.0  y* = 6.0
spend on x = 12.0  spend on y = 12.0  total = 24.0
s4 check expr (1/2)*(24/4) = 3.0
```

Tangency `y/x = 2` gives `y = 2x`; the budget `4x + 2(2x) = 24` gives `8x = 24`,
so `x* = 3`, `y* = 6`, spending exactly 24 (12 on each good). This matches the
boxed `answer` (3, 6). The `check:` block `(1/2) * (24 / 4)` returns `3.0` — this
is `x* = (a/(a+b))·m/px` with `a = b = 1`, i.e. `(1/2)·(24/4)` (value 3, tol 1%).
`ok`.

## Problem `s5` — spending-share shortcut, U = x^3 y

```python
# U = x^3 y (a=3, b=1), px=5, m=40
a, b = 3, 1
px, m = 5, 40
share_x = a/(a+b)
spend_x = share_x * m
x_star = spend_x / px
print("share on x = a/(a+b) =", share_x)
print("spending on x =", spend_x)
print("x* =", x_star)
print("s5 check expr (3/4)*40/5 =", (3/4) * 40 / 5)
```

output:

```
share on x = a/(a+b) = 0.75
spending on x = 30.0
x* = 6.0
s5 check expr (3/4)*40/5 = 6.0
```

The share on x is `3/(3+1) = 3/4`; `(3/4)·40 = 30` spent on x; `30/5 = 6` units.
This matches the boxed `answer2` (x* = 6). The `check:` block `(3/4) * 40 / 5`
returns `6.0` (value 6, tol 1%). The Cobb-Douglas share rule is a standard micro
result, not OpenStax; the numbers are our own. `ok`.
