# Fact-check — MA-12

Lesson MA-12 assembles the **IS-LM model**: it draws the IS curve (goods market,
MA-10) and the LM curve (money market, MA-11) on one interest-rate / output
plane and reads their crossing as the simultaneous short-run equilibrium
(Y*, r*). It then works the two policy levers — a fiscal expansion shifts the IS
curve right (output and the interest rate both rise, the rise in the rate being
crowding out), a monetary expansion shifts the LM curve right (output rises but
the rate falls). Two worked problems follow: a baseline equilibrium (IS:
r = 10 − Y, LM: r = Y → Y* = r* = 5) and a fiscal expansion (IS': r = 12 − Y →
Y* = r* = 6, up from 5).

Source: OpenStax *Principles of Economics 3e*, CC BY 4.0 — §28.4 *Monetary
Policy and Economic Outcomes* (file 328), Appendix D *The Expenditure-Output
Model* ("Equilibrium in the Keynesian Cross Model", file 431; "The Multiplier
Effect", file 432), with Ch 25 §25.1 for the aggregate-demand components.

## IMPORTANT distinction — what is OpenStax and what is our construction

This follows the OpenStax-vs-standard split established in MA-05, MA-10 (IS
curve) and MA-11 (LM curve). MA-12 is the **synthesis** of those two curves, and
the synthesis — the IS-LM model itself — is the standard intermediate-macro
layer, built on OpenStax Principles foundations. Nothing is copied.

**FROM OPENSTAX Principles 3e** (cited with quotes + pages below) — the economic
content each curve and each policy carries:

- *Goods-market equilibrium: spending equals output, Y = C + I + G* (the
  Keynesian cross). 'The point where the aggregate expenditure line that is
  constructed from C + I + G + X – M crosses the 45-degree line will be the
  equilibrium for the economy. It is the only point on the aggregate expenditure
  line where the total amount being spent on aggregate demand equals the total
  level of production' (file 431, p.887); 'Equilibrium occurs where AE = Y'
  (p.889). Grounds `isEq`. The four AD components C + I + G + net exports: §25.1
  (p.611).

- *The money market and monetary policy — the central bank sets the money
  supply and thereby interest rates.* 'through open market operations the central
  bank changes bank reserves in a way which affects the supply curve of loanable
  funds. As a result, Figure 28.5 shows that interest rates change' (file 328,
  §The Effect of Monetary Policy on Interest Rates, p.679); the federal funds
  market 'with supply, demand, and a price' (§28.3, p.677). Grounds `lmEq`.

- *Money demand rises with income/transactions and falls with the interest
  rate.* 'with higher interest rates, it is relatively more attractive to put
  those funds in a financial investment than to make an investment in physical
  capital' (file 328, §The Effect of Monetary Policy on Aggregate Demand,
  p.680). Grounds the LM slope carried into `lmEq` (see MA-11).

- *Fiscal policy — government spending and taxes affect output.* 'The
  appropriate response to a recessionary gap is for the government to reduce
  taxes or increase spending so that the aggregate expenditure function shifts up
  from AE0 to AE1. When this shift occurs, the new equilibrium E1 now occurs at
  potential GDP' (file 431, p.891); the $100 government-spending increase lifting
  GDP through the multiplier (file 432, p.892-893). Grounds the fiscal-expansion
  content of `given2` / Problem s5.

- *Monetary policy affects interest rates and output.* 'An expansionary monetary
  policy will shift the supply of loanable funds to the right... leading to an
  equilibrium (E1) with a lower 6% interest rate' (file 328, Fig 28.5, p.679);
  'expansionary monetary policy will reduce interest rates and stimulate
  investment and consumption spending, causing the original aggregate demand
  curve (AD0) to shift right to AD1' (file 328, p.680). So a monetary expansion
  lowers the rate and raises output — the opposite sign on rates from a fiscal
  expansion, exactly as the lesson claims. Grounds the s3 monetary-policy
  narration.

- *Crowding out — qualitatively.* OpenStax Principles notes that higher interest
  rates discourage private investment ('Business investment will decline because
  it is less attractive for firms to borrow money', file 328, p.680) and, in its
  fiscal-policy chapter, that government borrowing can raise interest rates and
  reduce private investment. §28.4 does NOT contain the phrase 'crowding out' in
  the extracted text, so the *named* crowding-out effect read off a higher r* at
  the IS-LM crossing is marked **standard** below.

**STANDARD INTERMEDIATE-MACRO CONSTRUCTION — NOT in OpenStax Principles 3e.**
Flagged clearly, built by us FROM the OpenStax foundations above:

- the **IS-LM model** itself — the IS and LM curves drawn on one r–Y plane
  (`eqEq`, the s2 diagram `drawIS` / `drawLM` / `mark`);
- their **intersection as the simultaneous equilibrium** (Y*, r*) — `eqEq`,
  `eqEq2`, and the s4 solve;

OpenStax Principles builds **each market separately** — the Keynesian cross for

policy effects off a single crossing of the two curves.** IS-LM is the standard
synthesis of MA-10 and MA-11. The economics is OpenStax; the model that assembles
it is our standard-macro layer.

## Note on crowding out (`answer2`)

The lesson reads the equilibrium interest rate rising (5 → 6) after the fiscal
expansion as **crowding out**: the higher rate chokes off some private
investment. The underlying mechanism — higher interest rates discourage business
investment — is OpenStax ('Business investment will decline because it is less
attractive for firms to borrow money', file 328, p.680), and OpenStax's
fiscal-policy chapter discusses government borrowing raising rates and reducing
private investment qualitatively. The extracted §28.4 text does not use the term
'crowding out', and OpenStax never presents it through an IS-LM diagram, so
reading crowding out off a higher r* at the new crossing is marked **standard
intermediate-macro** (flagged in `answer2`). The economics is OpenStax-consistent;
the IS-LM reading is our layer.
