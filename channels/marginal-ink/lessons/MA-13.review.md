# Fact-check — MA-13

Lesson MA-13 builds the aggregate demand / aggregate supply (AD-AS) model: a
downward-sloping aggregate demand curve, an upward-sloping short-run aggregate
supply curve, their intersection as the economy's equilibrium output and price
level, and a vertical long-run aggregate supply curve at potential GDP. It then
works the shocks — a demand shock shifts AD so output and the price level move
the same way (boom/recession); a supply shock shifts SRAS so they move opposite
ways, and an adverse supply shock gives higher prices with lower output, i.e.
stagflation — and closes with the long-run return to potential output. Two
worked problems use our own illustrative linear curves.

Source: OpenStax *Principles of Economics 3e*, Ch 24 The Aggregate Demand/
Aggregate Supply Model — §24.2 "Building a Model of Aggregate Demand and
Aggregate Supply", §24.3 "Shifts in Aggregate Supply", §24.4 "Shifts in
Aggregate Demand", CC BY 4.0.

## This lesson is DIRECTLY from OpenStax

Every qualitative claim on the board in MA-13 is standard Principles content
stated explicitly in OpenStax Ch 24 — the AD/SRAS/LRAS shapes, the equilibrium
at the intersection, demand shocks moving output and prices together, supply
shocks moving them apart, stagflation, and the long-run return to potential.
**Only the two problems' linear curves (AD: P = 10 − Y, SRAS: P = Y; shifted
AD': P = 14 − Y) are our own simple illustrative parameterisation**, chosen to
make the intersection solvable by hand. OpenStax itself uses an illustrative
numeric table (the fictional country Xurbia, Table 24.1) solved for an
intersection equilibrium, so a solvable numeric example is in keeping with the
source; our specific numbers are our own choice, not copied.

## s3 narration — demand shock (same direction), supply shock (opposite), stagflation, long run

Demand shock — output and the price level move the **same** way:

> "When AD shifts to the right, the new equilibrium (E1) will have a higher
> quantity of output and also a higher price level compared with the original
> equilibrium (E0)." (§24.4 Figure 24.8a caption, p.594; source lines 105-106)

> "When AD shifts to the left, the new equilibrium (E1) will have a lower
> quantity of output and also a lower price level." (§24.4 Figure 24.8b caption,
> p.594; source lines 108-110)

Supply shock — output and the price level move in **opposite** directions:

> "The new equilibrium, E1, has a reduced quantity of output and a higher price
> level than the original equilibrium (E0)." (§24.3 Figure 24.7b caption, p.591;
> source lines 63-64)

An adverse supply shock gives higher prices + lower output = **stagflation**:

> "of E1 will bring a nasty set of effects: reduced GDP or recession, higher
> unemployment because the economy is now further away from potential GDP, and
> an inflationary higher price level as well... In the 1970s, this pattern of a
> shift to the left in SRAS leading to a stagnant economy with high unemployment
> and inflation was nicknamed stagflation." (§24.4, p.592; source lines 3-7)

Long run — the economy is pinned to potential output:

> "In the long run, however, producers are limited to producing at potential
> GDP." (§24.3, p.590; source lines 29-30)

The s3 point that a supply shock is a genuine policy dilemma (no AD shift fixes
both the slump and the inflation) follows directly from the above: an adverse
SRAS shift raises prices while lowering output, so moving AD to fight one worsens
the other. The growth point (LRAS marches rightward as potential output expands)
is §24.3 Figure 24.7a: "full employment corresponds to a higher level of
potential GDP, which we show as a rightward shift in LRAS" (p.590, source lines
56-57). All consistent with source. `ok`.
