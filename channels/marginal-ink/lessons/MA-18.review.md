# Fact-check — MA-18

## Source status (FLAGGED — read first)

Intro OpenStax (*Principles of Economics 3e*) does **not** present the formal
Mundell-Fleming model or write the uncovered-interest-parity condition as an
equation. The lesson header (lines 2–6) states this honestly. This review
documents it exactly as done for MA-15 (Taylor rule) and MA-16 (budget
constraint):

- The **three regimes and their tradeoffs** — floating rate, hard peg / merged
  currency, and capital controls — **are** described in OpenStax §29.4, and the
  **interest-rate mechanism** that drives the choice (capital chasing the higher
  return, moving the exchange rate) **is** described in §29.2. Those passages are
  quoted verbatim below.
- The **algebraic parity condition** (`i = i* + Δeᵉ`, `Δeᵉ = 0` under a credible
  peg, `i = i*`) is the **standard open-economy identity / Mundell-Fleming
  construction**. Intro OpenStax states the economics in words but does not write
  the equation. Each such id is marked `ok` with this NOTE and the closest
  supporting prose quoted.

No economic claim in the lesson contradicts the source: every mechanism the
lesson asserts (rate differentials move capital and the exchange rate; a peg
with open capital surrenders monetary independence; a merged currency gives up
domestic monetary policy; capital controls are the only way to keep a fixed rate
*and* an independent rate) is literally stated in OpenStax §29.2/§29.4.

## Equation `parityEq`  —  `i = i^* + \Delta e^e`

NOTE: standard uncovered-interest-parity relation / Mundell-Fleming trilemma;
intro OpenStax states the regime tradeoffs and the rate-differential mechanism
in words (quoted), the algebraic parity condition is the standard construction,
flagged in the lesson header. The closest supporting OpenStax prose — that
interest-rate differences drive capital flows and hence the exchange rate:

> "The motivation for investment, whether domestic or foreign, is to earn a
> return. If rates of return in a country look relatively high, then that
> country will tend to attract funds from abroad. Conversely, if rates of return
> in a country look relatively low, then funds will tend to flee to other
> economies." — §29.2, *Differences across Countries in Rates of Return*, p.706

> "a higher interest rate or rate of return relative to other countries leads a
> nation's currency to appreciate or strengthen, and a lower interest rate
> relative to other countries leads a nation's currency to depreciate or
> weaken." — §29.2, p.706

The `Δeᵉ` (expected depreciation) term is grounded in §29.2's expectations
mechanism:

> "One reason to demand a currency on the foreign exchange market is the belief
> that the currency's value is about to increase. One reason to supply a
> currency … is the expectation that the currency's value is about to decline."
> — §29.2, *Expectations about Future Exchange Rates*, p.705

## Equation `fixedEq`  —  `fixed ⇒ Δeᵉ = 0`

NOTE: standard Mundell-Fleming construction (a *credible* fixed rate implies zero
expected change); intro OpenStax states it in words, the algebra is the standard
construction, flagged in the header.

> "With a hard peg exchange rate policy, the central bank sets a fixed and
> unchanging value for the exchange rate." — §29.4, *Using Soft Pegs and Hard
> Pegs*, p.713

> Table 29.3 — "Large short-run fluctuations in exchange rates? … Hard Peg:
> None, unless a change in the fixed rate … Merged Currency: None" — §29.4,
> *Tradeoffs of Exchange Rate Policies*, p.716

A credibly fixed rate therefore carries no expected change, i.e. `Δeᵉ = 0`.

## Equation `pinnedEq`  —  `i = i^*`

NOTE: standard Mundell-Fleming construction; intro OpenStax states the loss of
monetary independence under a hard peg with open capital in words (quoted), the
algebra `i = i*` is the standard construction, flagged in the header.

> "With a hard peg policy, domestic monetary policy is effectively no longer
> determined by domestic inflation or unemployment, but only by what monetary
> policy is needed to keep the exchange rate at the hard peg." — §29.4,
> *Tradeoffs of Soft Pegs and Hard Pegs*, p.714

> "Like a hard peg, a merged currency means that a nation has given up altogether
> on domestic monetary policy, and instead has put its interest rate policies in
> other hands." — §29.4, *A Merged Currency*, p.715

Combining `parityEq` with `fixedEq` gives `i = i*`: the home rate is pinned to
the world rate — the left edge of the triangle (fixed rate + free capital ⇒ no
monetary independence).

## Problem `s4`

```python
# s4: credible peg with open capital. World rate given; find required home rate.
# Interest parity: i = i* + Delta e^e. Credible peg => Delta e^e = 0.
i_star = 4      # world interest rate, %
delta_e = 0     # expected depreciation under a credible peg
i = i_star + delta_e
print("i = i* + Delta e^e =", i_star, "+", delta_e, "=", i, "%")
# check: block expr "4 + 0", value 4, tol 0.01

```

## Problem `s5`

```python
# s5: floating currency, home rate above world rate. Find expected depreciation.
# Rearranged parity: Delta e^e = i - i*.
i = 7           # home interest rate, %
i_star = 4      # world interest rate, %
delta_e = i - i_star
print("Delta e^e = i - i* =", i, "-", i_star, "=", delta_e, "%")
# check: block expr "7 - 4", value 3, tol 0.01
print("check expr 7-4 =", 7 - 4, "| boxed value = 3 | within tol:", abs((7-4) - 3) <= 0.01)
```

output:
```
Delta e^e = i - i* = 7 - 4 = 3 %
check expr 7-4 = 3 | boxed value = 3 | within tol: True
```

Economics sound: a currency paying 3 percentage points above the world rate must
be one markets expect to lose about 3% of its value — the interest premium just
compensates holders for the anticipated depreciation (§29.2, p.705). A high rate
is not "free money".
